# CHAPTER 1: HOW TO PLAY

## Welcome to the Long Night

Blood & Bone is a game about monsters trying to hold on to something human, and humans trying to survive a world where monsters are real. You'll play a Vampire starving in a city of strangers, a Werewolf torn between pack and self, a Hunter one bad night from becoming the thing they swore to destroy — or any of the other Kinds who walk these pages. Every session, you'll make choices. Every choice has a cost. This chapter tells you how the game actually works at the table.

## The Three Pillars

Every session of Blood & Bone rests on three pillars. Keep them in mind and the rest of the game falls into place.

**1. Fiction First.** Nothing happens mechanically until it happens in the story. You don't "roll Bone" in a vacuum — you describe your character slamming a door shut against a pursuing hunter, and *that action* is what triggers a move. The dice serve the fiction; the fiction is never just an excuse to roll dice.

**2. Move-Driven Play.** You don't choose to make a move the way you'd choose a spell from a list. Moves are triggered — when the fiction says you're doing something a move covers, that move happens, whether you meant to trigger it or not. Learning to recognize your triggers is the core skill of playing this game well.

**3. Consequence Without Cruelty.** Blood & Bone is not interested in punishing you for playing your character. Stress, Burdens, broken Connections — these exist to generate *story*, not to make you feel bad for existing. A miss isn't a failure state to dread; it's the game handing you a complication worth playing out.

## What Is a Move?

A move is a rule that tells you what happens when your character does something with an uncertain or risky outcome. Moves cover everything from feeding on a victim (*Sate the Beast*) to surviving a fight (*Resolve the Skirmish*) to the long, ruinous stretch of time when your character loses themselves entirely (*Surrender to the Years*).

Most moves fall into one of two categories:

- **Action Moves** ask you to make an Action Roll — the three-dice roll described below. These cover things where success and failure both matter and both are interesting: a fight, a feeding, a desperate escape, a moment of persuasion.
- **Progress Moves** don't use the Action Roll at all. They resolve automatically based on a condition being met — reaching maximum Stress, filling your Ledger, completing a long-term goal. You don't decide to trigger these; the fiction and your character sheet do.

You never need to hunt through the book to figure out what to roll. If you're not sure whether a move applies, describe what your character is doing and let the fiction tell you. If nothing covers it, you probably don't need to roll at all — just say what happens.

## The Flow of Play

You see the night through your character. Most of play is saying what they do next and why: chasing a scent, keeping a promise, staying ahead of whatever is chasing them. Your hunger, your vows and the people you still hold on to will keep pulling you somewhere. When what you do is something a move covers, you turn to the move.

If the move calls for dice, roll them. If it doesn't, it simply happens.

- A **strong hit:** the moment is yours. You got what you went for, and you choose what happens next.
- A **weak hit:** you get it, but not cleanly. Something costs you or comes back at you, and your next move answers it instead of choosing freely.
- A **miss:** it goes wrong. The situation turns against you, and you'll be dealing with what it cost.

## The Action Roll, In Depth

Most moves call for the action roll: three dice thrown together whenever your character tries something that could go badly.

- **Action Die:** One six-sided die (D6)
- **Challenge Dice:** Two ten-sided dice (D10)

The move names the stat to add to the six-sided die ("roll +Shadow"), sometimes offering two to choose from. Circumstances can grant small extra bonuses, called adds ("add +1").

**Adds are capped at +2 total per roll.** However many assets, allies, or advantages apply to a single action, they never combine to more than +2. This keeps the dice meaningful for specialists — a character built around one kind of problem should be *reliably good* at it, not mathematically incapable of failing.

Your **action score** is the six-sided die plus the stat plus your adds. It tops out at 10: any higher total still counts as 10.

Here's how to actually run one at the table, start to finish.

**Step 1 — Trigger the move.** Something happens in the fiction that a move covers. The GM (or the move's text) tells you which stat to add, or gives you a choice between two.

**Step 2 — Gather your adds.** Adds are situational bonuses — help from an ally, an advantage you set up earlier, a piece of gear suited to the task. Not every roll has adds. Don't go looking for them; let the fiction justify them. Remember the cap: +2 total, no matter how many sources apply.

*When a character over-covers a task* — three separate assets all pointing at the same roll — that's usually a signal not to roll at all. A Hunter with high Wits and two investigation assets shouldn't roll to learn a Kind's Weakness; they simply know it. Give it to them and save the dice for what the archive can't answer. If you'd rather keep the roll, hand the surplus back as *effect* instead of odds: on a strong hit they ask two questions instead of one, or learn a creature's Weakness and its Archetype together.

**Step 3 — Roll all three dice together.** Action Die + two Challenge Dice, all at once. Add your stat and any adds to the Action Die to get your Action Score. Remember: your Action Score caps at 10. Rolling a 6 on your Action Die with a Bone of 3 and an add of +2 doesn't get you an 11 — it gets you a 10.

**Step 4 — Compare and resolve.** Check your Action Score against each Challenge Die separately.

- Beat both dice → **Strong Hit**
- Beat one die → **Weak Hit**
- Beat neither → **Miss**

Remember: ties always go to the Challenge Dice. If your Action Score equals a Challenge Die, that die counts as *not* beaten.

**Step 5 — Play it out.** The move's text tells you what a strong hit, weak hit, or miss means specifically. Narrate it. Let the numbers become story.

### Resolving the Outcome

Hold your action score up against each ten-sided die on its own. Only a higher score beats a die; a tie belongs to the die.

- **Strong Hit:** you beat both challenge dice. You do what you set out to do.
- **Weak Hit:** you beat one of them. You do it, but it costs you or falls short.
- **Miss:** you beat neither. It fails, or you give up something serious to keep it going.

### A Note on Doubles

If both Challenge Dice show the same number — a "match" — something happens beyond the normal outcome. On a matched strong hit, treat it as an unusually clean, memorable success: the kind of moment other characters will talk about later. On a matched miss, the situation doesn't just go wrong — it goes wrong in a way that escalates immediately, often introducing a new complication on top of Paying the Price. GMs, use matches as your cue to make something *notable* happen, good or bad.

## Paying the Price

A miss usually ends with Pay the Price: the night takes something. The scene gets worse, a new danger arrives, or you lose something you'll feel — sometimes in the story alone, sometimes on your sheet as well.

**Every move in this book has its own miss oracle** — a d6 table of consequences specific to what that move was trying to do. They're gathered in Chapter 16. Roll one when you don't already know what a miss means, and treat the result as a prompt to make specific rather than an instruction to follow literally.

## Fictional Positioning

Not every action is created equal, and Blood & Bone expects the GM and players to talk about *why* a roll is happening before the dice hit the table.

Ask yourself:

- **Is this actually risky?** If a Vampire with decades of practice wants to blend into a crowded bar, that's not a roll — that's just true. Save the dice for moments where failure is genuinely possible and interesting.
- **What's actually at stake?** Before you roll, you and the GM should have a rough sense of what a miss would cost. If nobody can articulate what's at risk, the roll probably isn't necessary yet — get closer to the danger first.
- **Does the fictional setup earn you anything?** A character who spent a scene stalking their prey through the shadows has earned different positioning than one who charged in blind. The GM can grant an add, allow a different stat, or in extreme cases skip the roll for an automatic weak hit if you've set things up well enough.

Fictional positioning is a conversation, not a formula. If it feels like a roll matters, it probably does. If it feels like a foregone conclusion, say so and move on.

## Not Everything Is a Move

The single most common mistake new tables make with this system is rolling too much. Blood & Bone will grind to a halt, and stop feeling dangerous, if every action gets a die roll. Most of what your character does should just happen, narrated, with no dice at all.

**A roll should mean something is genuinely in question.** If the outcome is obvious, don't roll — just say what happens.

- If a Werewolf mid-transformation is grappling an ordinary, unarmed mortal, there's no roll. The mortal doesn't have a chance, and pretending otherwise with dice cheapens what the Werewolf actually is. Just narrate it: the mortal goes down, or runs, or freezes. Save the roll for when something real is at stake — the mortal has a weapon, there are witnesses, someone the Werewolf cares about is watching.
- A Wendigo whose mask has slipped doesn't need to roll to scare someone. Terror is automatic. What might need a roll is what happens *next* — do they get away clean, or does someone recognize them later, or does a witness manage to get a photo before fleeing?
- A century-old Vampire trying to charm a bored bartender into a free drink isn't a roll. That's just true, the same way a professional athlete doesn't roll to win a footrace against an untrained stranger. Rolls exist for moments where the outcome could plausibly go either way and both outcomes are interesting.

**Don't stack rolls for the same beat.** If you just rolled to break down a door, you don't then roll again to walk through it, and again to cross the room. One roll typically covers a whole beat of fictional action, not each individual step within it. If a player rolled moments ago to escape a chasing mob, and now wants to duck into an alley to catch their breath, that's the fictional consequence of the last roll playing out — not a fresh trigger.

**A good gut check before calling for dice:**

1. Is the outcome actually uncertain, given who this character is and what they can do?
2. Did something already get rolled for this exact beat of action?
3. Would failure here be interesting, or would it just be an arbitrary "no"?

If the answer to any of these is "no, not really," skip the dice and narrate the outcome directly. GMs should feel free to simply say "that just works" or "that's clearly beyond you" without ever touching the dice — that's not a shortcut, it's the system working as intended. Save your Challenge Dice for moments that deserve them, and those moments will hit much harder.

## Roles at the Table

**The Players** drive their characters' choices, hungers, and relationships. You decide what your character wants and how far they're willing to go to get it. You are never required to make your character behave well — Blood & Bone is more interesting when you don't.

**The GM (Game Master)** doesn't roll dice against you. Instead, the GM's job is to:

- Frame scenes and introduce complications, factions, and threats.
- Ask questions that reveal character (*"What does your Beast want you to do right now?"*).
- Play NPCs, monsters, and the city itself as a living, reactive thing.
- Never fudge outcomes — the dice are the dice, hit or miss, and the GM's job is to make every result matter.

**GM Principles** — a short list to keep the table honest:

1. Make the world feel alive and indifferent to the characters' humanity.
2. Play monsters as people and people as potential monsters.
3. Ask questions; build on the answers.
4. Let Stress, Burdens, and Connections do the heavy narrative lifting — don't invent punishment outside the system.
5. Never let a miss be *just* a failure. It's always a door to somewhere new.

## Session Zero

Before you play your first session, sit down as a table and answer a few questions together:

- **What kind of city is this?** A sprawling metropolis where monsters hide in plain sight? A dying rust-belt town where everyone already suspects something's wrong? Agree on tone and scale.
- **How do the Kinds coexist?** Is there an uneasy truce between Kinds? Open warfare? Total ignorance of one another? This shapes every relationship at the table.
- **What are your character's Connections, going in?** Who do they still have, and what's fraying?
- **What lines does the table not cross?** Blood & Bone deals in predation, violence, and moral collapse. Agree up front on what's off-limits and how to signal discomfort during play. Use whatever safety tools your table prefers — this book assumes you'll bring your own.
- **Choose your Cohort.** Decide why these characters are together before anyone builds one (Chapter 7, Step Zero). A group with no shared reason to cooperate will come apart the first time someone's hunger is inconvenient.
- **Compare sheets before you begin.** Look at the whole group's stats together. If no one has a given stat above 1, that's a real, shared blind spot — most often Bone, since few players want to be the brawler in a horror game. Don't fix it by rebuilding characters. Name it out loud, and let the GM build threats that press on it. A group that cannot fight is a group that has to run, negotiate, or outthink, and that's a better game than one where every problem has an answer on someone's sheet.

## An Example of Play

*GM:* "You're three nights into starvation, Mira. Your Stress is sitting at 4. You catch the scent of a jogger a block ahead, alone, headphones in. What do you do?"

*Player (playing Mira, a Vampire):* "I follow her. Not to feed yet — just to see how isolated I can get her."

*GM:* "That's reasonable positioning — you're not committing to anything risky yet, just closing distance. She turns down an alley to cut through to her building. This is your moment. Do you take it?"

*Player:* "I do. I come up behind her, quiet, and go for the feed. That's Sate the Beast, right? I'll roll +Shadow — I want this clean."

*(Player rolls Action Die + Shadow against two Challenge Dice. Action Score of 7. Challenge Dice show 4 and 9.)*

*GM:* "You beat the 4, not the 9 — that's a weak hit. You get the feed, Stress drops to 3. But things get messy: as you're finishing, a car turns onto the street, headlights sweeping the alley. Someone might have seen something. What do you do?"

Notice what happened here: no roll occurred until the fiction earned one. The weak hit didn't just reduce a number — it produced a complication the GM could build an entire session around. That's Blood & Bone working as intended.

## Quick Reference: Running a Move

1. Something happens in the fiction.
2. Identify the move it triggers (or recognize that no roll is needed).
3. Roll if it's an Action Move; otherwise resolve the Progress Move directly.
4. Compare the Action Score to both Challenge Dice.
5. Narrate the strong hit, weak hit, or miss according to the move's text.
6. Let the outcome change the fiction going forward — new complications, new Stress, new Burdens, new Connections gained or lost.

The rest of this book is built on this loop, repeated across feeding, fighting, fleeing, and the slow, inevitable slide toward losing yourself entirely. Everything from here is detail. This chapter is the engine.

# CHAPTER 2: THE CHARACTER

## Core Stats

Five stats describe what your character is good at and how they tend to solve problems. Each is rated from 1 to 3.

- **Nerve:** quickness, guns, getting out of the way, keeping your head when everything moves at once, striking on instinct.
- **Heart:** resolve, compassion, how you reach people, and the humanity you are still holding on to.
- **Bone:** raw strength, taking punishment, and violence at arm's length or closer.
- **Shadow:** moving unseen, lying well, setting traps for people, hunting from the dark.
- **Wits:** what you know, what you notice, and thinking faster than whoever is across from you.

## Supernatural Kinds

The monster you are dictates how you survive the modern nights. Each Kind has a unique feeding requirement, experience triggers, signature mechanic, and weakness.

### The Vampire

Driven by an unyielding blood addiction, vampires view mortals as cattle, blending into society to hunt.

- **Stress Escalation:** Increases when starved, when smelling fresh blood, or when exposed to direct sunlight.
- **Recover Move: Sate the Beast** – Requires drinking mortal blood.
- **Progression Triggers (XP):** 1 XP for feeding successfully without detection; 3 XP for draining a human completely dry.
- **Weakness:** Sunlight is lethal, not just uncomfortable — direct exposure causes escalating physical harm the longer they're caught in it, not merely Stress. Additionally, a vampire cannot cross a mortal threshold uninvited, no matter how dire the need.

### The Gargoyle

Monstrous physical specimens forced into isolation. They possess terrifying strength and wings, but their grotesque appearance shatters the masquerade.

- **Stress Escalation:** Increases when exposed to mortals, when their disguise slips, or when the sun begins to rise.
- **Recover Move: Sate the Beast** – Requires claiming absolute dominion over a hidden territory or terrifying a mortal into silence.
- **Progression Triggers (XP):** 1 XP for navigating a mortal space completely undetected; 3 XP for establishing a new, perfectly secure haven; 5 XP for eliminating a direct threat to their secrecy.
- **Sunlight Mechanic (Stone Slumber):** If caught in sunlight, Gargoyles instantly turn to stone. They are impervious to environmental hazards but entirely helpless against targeted destruction.
- **Weakness:** Stone Slumber is involuntary and total. Once sunlight triggers it, a Gargoyle has zero agency until nightfall — no rolls, no reactions, no defense. Anyone who knows their location during the day has a free window to destroy them.

### The Wendigo

Once human, now driven by a traumatic, cannibalistic curse. They can pass perfectly as human — but only as long as they are well-fed on human flesh.

- **Stress Escalation:** Increases when exposed to extreme cold, when witnessing mortal vulnerability, or when starving.
- **Recover Move: Sate the Beast** – Requires consuming human flesh.
- **Progression Triggers (XP):** 1 XP for consuming preserved/scavenged human flesh; 3 XP for hunting and consuming a living human; 5 XP for consuming another supernatural creature.
- **The Slipping Mask:** If a Wendigo starves, their human disguise drops, revealing the monster beneath. Feeding restores their human appearance.
- **Weakness:** The hunger only grows. Each time a Wendigo feeds, the threshold for the next Stress spike gets lower — meaning veteran Wendigos need to feed more often, not less, just to stay stable. There is no long-term equilibrium, only a slower or faster slide.

### The Werewolf

Bound to the moon and their own rage, werewolves fight for dominance as much as survival. Pack is everything — a lone wolf is a dying wolf.

- **Stress Escalation:** Increases when isolated from the pack, when challenged for dominance, or as the moon waxes toward full.
- **Recover Move: Sate the Beast** – Requires a physical release of dominance: winning a fight, a hunt, or a challenge within the pack hierarchy.
- **Progression Triggers (XP):** 1 XP for defending pack territory or a packmate; 3 XP for winning a dominance challenge; 5 XP for successfully leading the pack through a crisis.
- **The Moon's Pull:** On the night of the full moon, all Stress Escalation triggers apply automatically, regardless of circumstance. Resisting the Change that night requires a roll +Heart; on a miss, you turn involuntarily.
- **Weakness:** Silver wounds cannot be healed by supernatural recovery — only time and mundane medicine close them, and they scar permanently.

### The Witch/Warlock

Once mortal, now bound to power that was never meant for human hands. Magic exacts a price for every favor, and the debt always comes due.

- **Stress Escalation:** Increases when a working goes wrong, when the source of their power grows displeased, or when they're cut off from their tools (grimoire, coven, sacred space).
- **Recover Move: Sate the Beast** – Requires performing a ritual sacrifice — of blood, of a possession, or of someone else's secret.
- **Progression Triggers (XP):** 1 XP for successfully casting a working beyond their normal limits; 3 XP for binding a new source of power (a spirit, a pact, a relic); 5 XP for breaking or renegotiating a pact that should have been unbreakable.
- **The Ledger:** Every act of magic is tallied. When the Ledger fills, the source of the witch's power calls in its due — the GM introduces a complication or demand that must be answered before further workings are possible.
- **Weakness:** Their power is tied to a physical anchor (grimoire, familiar, working circle). Destroy or steal it, and the witch is reduced to a mortal until it's recovered.

### The Hunter

Not a monster — the thing that hunts monsters. No claws, no fangs, just training, grit, and a growing certainty that the line between predator and prey is thinner than they'd like.

- **Stress Escalation:** Increases when a hunt goes wrong, when they're forced to work with (or spare) a monster, or when they see how close they've come to becoming what they hunt.
- **Recover Move: Steel the Resolve** – Requires confiding in a trusted ally, returning to a place of safety, or completing a hunt clean. Roll +Heart (if leaning on connection) or +Wits (if leaning on discipline/routine).
  - Strong hit: decrease Stress by 2.
  - Weak hit: decrease Stress by 1, but an old wound (physical or otherwise) resurfaces.
  - Miss: your resolve cracks. Pay the Price.
- **Progression Triggers (XP):** 1 XP for successfully identifying and confirming a monster's Kind before engaging; 3 XP for taking down a supernatural threat without civilian casualties; 5 XP for showing mercy to a monster at real personal cost.
- **The Growing Doubt:** Hunters don't lose control to a beast — they lose control to despair and moral erosion. When Stress caps at 6, they don't transform; they break. (This is what Surrender to the Years already covers for them.)
- **Weakness:** No supernatural healing, no Sate the Beast shortcut. A Hunter's only recovery tools are mundane — time, medicine, and other people. This makes them fragile, but also means nothing can be taken from them the way a source of power can be taken from a Witch or a pack from a Werewolf.

> **Design Note: The Hunter is the Odd One Out.** Every other Kind in this book runs on the same chassis: a beast to feed, a Stress meter tied to that hunger, and a Sate the Beast move to buy relief at a cost. The Hunter breaks that pattern on purpose. They have no beast, no hunger, no supernatural recovery — their Stress comes from moral erosion and their Recover Move (Steel the Resolve) works through connection and discipline instead of predation. This is intentional: the Hunter is meant to feel *fragile and human* at a table full of monsters, and their Time Skip outcome (breaking under despair rather than transforming) reflects that. GMs and players should expect the Hunter's chapter to read differently from the rest — that's not an inconsistency to fix, it's the point. If future playtesting shows the Hunter needs their own dedicated moves beyond what the other Kinds share (Rule of 3, Surrender to the Years, etc.), that asymmetry should be preserved rather than smoothed over.

### The Ghost

Death didn't take. Something kept you tethered — unfinished business, a violent end, a promise unkept — and now you drift through a world that can barely see you, growing less human with every year you refuse to let go.

- **Stress Escalation:** Increases when confronted with the manner of their death, when their tether is threatened or ignored, or when they're forced to interact with the living world directly (crossing running water, entering hallowed ground, physical contact).
- **Recover Move: Sate the Beast** – Requires reinforcing the tether: revisiting the site of death, interacting with the person/object/unfinished business that binds them, or draining warmth/life force from something living.
- **Progression Triggers (XP):** 1 XP for influencing the living world without revealing yourself; 3 XP for advancing your unfinished business in a meaningful way; 5 XP for resolving — or permanently securing — your tether.
- **The Fading:** A Ghost who goes too long without reinforcing their tether begins to lose cohesion — memories, form, even sense of self. Mechanically, this can manifest as temporarily losing access to a stat or a Connection until the tether is reinforced.
- **Weakness:** A Ghost's tether is a specific, discoverable thing. Anyone who identifies and destroys, resolves, or claims it can force the Ghost to move on — permanently removing them from play — without ever needing to fight them.

## Kind Archetypes

Choosing a Kind tells you *what* you are. An Archetype tells you *how you carry it*. Choose one Archetype when you choose your Kind — it doesn't replace your Kind's core rules, it bends them.

Each Archetype also adds two sets of three XP triggers on top of your Kind's Progression Triggers. These reward playing your Archetype's specific relationship to the hunger — an Abstinent Vampire and a Ripper earn XP for nearly opposite behavior, and that's the point.

Each Archetype also lists three **Stress Relief** options: small, archetype-specific ways to take the edge off without a full feeding. Each may be used once per session, requires no roll, and decreases your Stress by 1. They cannot take you below Stress 1 — only Sate the Beast (or Steel the Resolve) can do that. These exist so a character can survive a session without hunting, at the cost of never fully clearing the meter.

**Archetype XP** triggers reward the act itself — the feeding, the hunt, the restraint, the working. **Alternate XP** triggers reward what that way of living builds around you: reputation, relationships, territory, the people who notice. Decide at Session Zero whether your table uses one set, the other, or both. Using both makes for faster advancement and a more powerful game; using one keeps progression tighter. Groups that want a more social, political chronicle should lean toward Alternate XP; groups that want a lean survival-horror game should lean toward Archetype XP.

### VAMPIRE

**The Abstinent** — *Feeds on animal blood, fighting the pull toward human prey.*
- Sate the Beast on animal blood: strong hit decreases Stress by only 1 (not 2) — it's incomplete nourishment.
- Never triggers a Mask Slips complication from feeding.
- +1 to any Heart roll made to resist the urge to feed on a human.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for sustaining yourself on animal blood through a full night of temptation; 3 XP for refusing human blood when feeding on it would have solved your problem outright; 5 XP for convincing another Vampire to attempt abstinence.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for maintaining a mortal relationship without them ever suspecting; 3 XP for being publicly mocked or threatened by other Vampires for your restraint and holding your position; 5 XP for saving a mortal from another Vampire's feeding.
- **Stress Relief:** a shared meal or ritual with a mortal who knows nothing; refusing an easy feed and sitting with the craving until it passes; tending to something living that isn't prey.

**The Ripper** — *Given fully to the addiction, feeding to excess.*
- Strong hit on Sate the Beast decreases Stress by 3 instead of 2.
- "The sight of blood" is an additional Stress Escalation trigger.
- A miss on Sate the Beast automatically includes the Mask Slips price, no choice involved.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for feeding to excess and walking away before killing; 3 XP for turning your appetite into leverage over someone who feared it; 5 XP for surviving a reckoning your feeding directly caused.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for feeding somewhere you had no business feeding; 3 XP for a mortal seeking you out despite knowing what you'd do; 5 XP for being hunted for your appetite and escaping clean.
- **Stress Relief:** feeding purely for pleasure rather than need; being feared by someone who once looked down on you; letting a victim live and watching them flee.

### GARGOYLE

**The Warden** — *Protects a hidden community without violence.*
- Sate the Beast via protecting territory: strong hit also removes one point of Stress from an ally's meter, if present.
- Terrorizing a mortal into silence no longer satisfies your Beast — only genuine protection does.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for shielding a mortal from harm without being seen; 3 XP for resolving a threat to your community without violence; 5 XP for revealing yourself to someone you protect, and being accepted.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for learning something personal about a mortal you protect; 3 XP for turning away another supernatural threat from your community; 5 XP for expanding your protection to somewhere new and holding it.
- **Stress Relief:** watching over your community through a full night undisturbed; repairing or improving something in your territory with your own hands; a mortal you protect being visibly, unknowingly safe.

**The Tyrant** — *Rules through fear and violent reputation.*
- Terrorizing mortals is more effective: strong hit on Sate the Beast this way decreases Stress by 3.
- Mortals who've encountered you are an additional Stress Escalation trigger when they resurface — your reputation follows you.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for a mortal obeying you purely on reputation, before you act; 3 XP for claiming territory from a rival through fear alone; 5 XP for breaking a faction's will without a single killing.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for a new rumor about you spreading without your involvement; 3 XP for a rival abandoning a claim rather than test you; 5 XP for a mortal institution — police, press, a landlord — quietly working around you.
- **Stress Relief:** a rival visibly deferring to you; walking your territory unchallenged; destroying something that belonged to someone who defied you.

### WENDIGO

**The Graverobber** — *Feeds on the already-dead, refusing to kill.*
- Consuming scavenged/preserved flesh decreases Stress by 2 on a strong hit (rather than the lesser feed it would otherwise be) — but The Slipping Mask advances one step faster between feedings, since it's a weaker feed.
- Never triggers Stress from "witnessing mortal vulnerability" — you've made peace with death, not violence.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for securing a feed from the already-dead without detection; 3 XP for building or protecting a reliable supply of scavenged flesh; 5 XP for holding to your rule through a starvation that nearly killed you.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for establishing a new source for scavenged flesh; 3 XP for keeping your rule secret from someone who'd use it against you; 5 XP for convincing another Wendigo to try your way.
- **Stress Relief:** restocking your stores to full; visiting a grave or memorial and paying respect to what you take; confessing what you are to someone who doesn't recoil.

**The Predator** — *Actively stalks and kills living prey.*
- Killing living prey decreases Stress by 2 and resets The Slipping Mask fully.
- Extreme cold triggers Stress escalation twice as easily — the curse recognizes you've leaned into it.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for a hunt that leaves no trace behind; 3 XP for taking prey from another predator's territory; 5 XP for hunting something that was hunting you.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for choosing prey by a standard of your own rather than convenience; 3 XP for a hunt that required genuine patience — days, not hours; 5 XP for hunting in territory that belonged to someone else and walking away uncontested.
- **Stress Relief:** a hunt that ends exactly as you planned it; running down something faster or stronger than you; a night spent in deep cold with nothing hunting you back.

### WEREWOLF

**The Packbound** — *Finds identity and stability entirely through the pack.*
- +1 to any roll made in direct defense of the pack or a packmate.
- Isolation from the pack triggers Stress escalation immediately, not gradually.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for putting a packmate's needs above your own at real cost; 3 XP for holding the pack together through a fracture that should have broken it; 5 XP for saving the pack at the price of your own standing within it.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for resolving a conflict between two packmates; 3 XP for bringing someone new into the pack successfully; 5 XP for the pack surviving a loss that should have scattered it.
- **Stress Relief:** a night spent with the pack doing nothing important; being touched, groomed, or physically close to a packmate; hearing a packmate acknowledge your place out loud.

**The Rogue** — *Self-exiled or cast out, surviving alone.*
- Immune to the "isolated from pack" Stress trigger — you've already made peace with being alone.
- Dominance challenges are harder for you to walk away from: a miss on Sate the Beast during one always adds a Doomed-track complication (a rival now hunts you specifically).
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for surviving a full moon alone without harming anyone; 3 XP for beating a pack-backed rival with no support of your own; 5 XP for building something lasting — a haven, an alliance, a reputation — entirely without a pack.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for refusing an offer to join a pack; 3 XP for being useful to a pack without belonging to it; 5 XP for another Rogue seeking you out for help.
- **Stress Relief:** a night with genuine solitude and no threats; marking new ground as your own; beating a challenger without any help arriving.

### WITCH/WARLOCK

**The Hedge Witch** — *Practices small, careful magic, avoiding large pacts.*
- Your Ledger fills slower — count only 1 mark per Sate the Beast ritual instead of escalating marks.
- Workings beyond your normal limits are harder: -1 to rolls attempting them.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for solving a problem with small magic that others would have needed a pact for; 3 XP for refusing an offer of greater power; 5 XP for undoing damage caused by someone else's reckless working.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for helping a mortal with something small and mundane; 3 XP for teaching restraint to someone chasing power; 5 XP for surviving direct pressure from a greater power without conceding anything.
- **Stress Relief:** a working done purely for someone else's benefit; a full night of study with no magic performed; tending your tools, garden, or workspace properly.

**The Servant** — *Chases power through increasingly reckless pacts.*
- Binding new sources of power (3 XP trigger) also grants an immediate +1 add to your next roll using that power.
- Your Ledger fills faster — 2 marks per ritual — and when it fills, the complication the GM introduces is guaranteed severe.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for successfully meeting a demand from your power's source; 3 XP for extracting more from a pact than its terms should allow; 5 XP for surviving a full Ledger without losing anything you couldn't replace.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for learning something new about what you're actually bound to; 3 XP for using your source's power to help someone other than yourself; 5 XP for making a second source want you, and choosing between them.
- **Stress Relief:** a demand from your source met promptly and fully; using borrowed power successfully and visibly; being recognized by another practitioner as something greater than they are.

### HUNTER

**The Zealot** — *A black-and-white crusader who sees monsters as monsters, full stop.*
- +1 to Steel the Resolve when it follows completing a hunt "clean" (no mercy shown).
- Showing mercy to a monster (5 XP trigger) instead costs you 1 Stress immediately, even though you still earn the XP — your convictions push back.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for completing a hunt exactly to doctrine, no deviations; 3 XP for holding to your convictions when someone you trust argued against them; 5 XP for eliminating a threat every other hunter had written off as impossible.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for warning a mortal away from something they didn't understand; 3 XP for recruiting or training someone new to the Vigil; 5 XP for exposing a monster that had successfully passed as human for years.
- **Stress Relief:** maintaining or cleaning your kit in full; a hunt that ended exactly according to doctrine; time spent among mortals who are safe because of you.

**The Pragmatist** — *Willing to work alongside monsters when it serves the greater good.*
- Can call on a monstrous Connection for aid using Steel the Resolve's +Heart option, something other Hunters can't do.
- Working with monsters no longer triggers Stress escalation on its own — only genuine betrayal or harm does.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for gaining useful information from a monster rather than a weapon; 3 XP for an alliance with a monster that saves mortal lives; 5 XP for changing another hunter's mind about where the line actually falls.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for keeping a monstrous ally's secret when it would have been easier not to; 3 XP for resolving a supernatural conflict without any deaths on either side; 5 XP for brokering a truce that holds.
- **Stress Relief:** an honest conversation with a monstrous Connection; a night where the threat resolved and nobody died; being trusted by someone who had no reason to trust you.

### GHOST

**The Anchored** — *Stable in their tether, still trying to help the living.*
- The Fading triggers only at a Stress of 5+ instead of gradually — you hold together longer.
- Influencing the living world without revealing yourself (1 XP trigger) is easier: +1 to the roll.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for helping a living person who never learns you exist; 3 XP for guiding another spirit toward resolution; 5 XP for protecting your tether's subject from serious harm.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for a living person acknowledging your presence willingly; 3 XP for preventing another spirit from harming the living; 5 XP for the person you protect finding real safety or peace.
- **Stress Relief:** time spent at your tether without threat or urgency; witnessing the person you protect happy and unaware; helping a living person in a way they notice, even if they don't understand.

**The Unraveling** — *Losing their grip, drifting toward predation.*
- Draining warmth/life force from the living decreases Stress by 3 on a strong hit (instead of 2).
- The Fading applies its penalty (losing access to a stat or Connection) at Stress 3+ instead of 5+ — you're burning yourself out faster in exchange for power.
- **Archetype XP:** 1 XP for taking warmth from the living without being noticed; 3 XP for using what you've taken to accomplish something you couldn't have alone; 5 XP for recovering a piece of yourself you'd already lost to the Fading.
- **Alternate XP:** 1 XP for realizing something about yourself you'd already forgotten; 3 XP for frightening away something that threatened what remains of your tether; 5 XP for someone still choosing to help you despite what you've become.
- **Stress Relief:** taking warmth from someone who won't be missed; frightening a living person badly enough that they flee; recovering a memory you thought was gone.

## The Turning

Every character at this table used to be something else. Sooner or later, someone will want to do to another person what was done to them — or a Hunter will cross a line they can't step back over. This is how that works.

### What Turning Costs

Making another creature is never a small act. It is, in most cities, the single most politically dangerous thing you can do: you're adding a mouth to a food supply that other people are already fighting over. The Concord treats unauthorized turning as a Concord violation. The Undersett kills for it. L'Atelier will not sell to you afterward.

Mechanically, turning someone always does three things:

1. **Increase your Exposure by 1.** Another creature exists who knows exactly what you are.
2. **Increase your Beast Meter by 1.** You've done the thing that was done to you, and something in you agreed to it.
3. **Create a Connection at Steady rank** with the person you turned, whether either of you wants it. It cannot be removed except by their destruction.

### THE MAKING

When you deliberately turn a mortal into your own Kind, roll +Heart (if they consented and you're trying to bring them through intact) or +Bone (if you're forcing it).

- **Strong hit:** They survive, and they come back *themselves*. They begin at Stress 2 and Beast 0, and they remember why they agreed — or that they didn't.
- **Weak hit:** They survive, but something is wrong. They begin at Beast 2, or they've lost something in the crossing — a memory, a relationship, a piece of who they were. You choose which.
- **Miss:** It goes badly. They die, or they come back as something that isn't what you intended and isn't stable — a Troublesome-to-Dangerous rank antagonist the GM now controls, with your face in its memory.

Not every Kind can be made. **Vampires** turn through blood. **Werewolves** turn by bite or by ritual. **Witches** are not turned but *initiated*, which uses this same move with the source of power as the other party. **Wendigos** are made by circumstance and starvation, not by another Wendigo's choice. **Gargoyles, Ghosts, and Hunters cannot be made at all** — Gargoyles are born or cursed, Ghosts are the result of a death that didn't take, and a Hunter is only ever something a person decides to become.

### The Crossing: When a PC Changes Kind

Sometimes it happens to a player character. A Hunter is turned. A dying Vampire's ghost refuses to go. A Witch's pact consumes them entirely.

When a player character becomes a different Kind, they do not roll a new character. Instead:

- **Choose the new Kind and a new Archetype.** Your old Kind's Weakness, Stress triggers, and Recover Move are replaced by the new ones immediately.
- **Keep your stats, Connections, Burdens, Conditions, Vows, and XP total.** You are the same person. You want the same things.
- **Keep every asset you've bought** — but Powers from your old Kind stop functioning. They aren't refunded; they're *lost*, and that loss should be felt. You may buy new Powers from your new Kind's list at normal cost.
- **Your Beast Meter does not reset.** Advance it by 2. Whatever you are now, you got here by losing ground.
- **Your Stress resets to 4.** You are new to this, and badly out of control.
- **Take a new Vow at Formidable rank or higher**, about what you are now.

### The Hunter's Fall

The most common crossing in this game is a Hunter becoming a monster, and it deserves its own note.

A Hunter who becomes another Kind does not lose the Hunter's Weakness — they gain a second one. They keep *no supernatural shortcut to recovery* as a scar of who they were: for that character, Sate the Beast recovers **1 less Stress** than it should, permanently, on every roll. Their body has changed. The part of them that refused this hasn't.

In exchange, they keep any Hunter Powers they'd bought. The Field Kit still works. Monster Lore still works, and now it works on them.

### Becoming a Hunter

A mortal becomes a Hunter by deciding to, and surviving the decision. There's no move for it, because there's nothing supernatural about it. It's just a person who found out, and didn't look away.

If a Companion, Connection, or NPC in your chronicle becomes a Hunter, they become a Hunter the same way any player character does — a Path, a Vow, and a very short life expectancy.

# CHAPTER 3: THE BEAST

## Stress and Hunger

Unlike mortals, your most dangerous enemy is yourself. You track this with two meters.

**Stress** is the short game. It runs from 0 to a maximum of **6**, rises through your Kind's specific triggers, and falls when you feed. When it caps out at 6, you trigger a Time Skip — a crisis you can come back from.

**The Beast Meter** is the long game. It runs from 0 to **10**, only ever goes up, and never resets. It measures how much of yourself you've permanently lost. At 10, you're gone for good.

The two are linked: the more Stress you carry, the faster the Beast Meter climbs. Feeding isn't just relief — it's the only brake you have on the slower, worse thing.

### SATE THE BEAST

When you perform your Kind's specific feeding or power-gathering ritual to reduce your stress, roll +Shadow (if hunting covertly) or +Bone (if taking it by force).

- On a **strong hit**, you satisfy the hunger. Decrease your stress meter by 2.
- On a **weak hit**, you feed, but things get messy. Decrease your stress meter by 1, and you must deal with a complication (a witness, a lingering body, a broken masquerade).
- On a **miss**, the beast takes over. You drain the victim dry, attack the wrong target, or lose yourself to the frenzy. Pay the Price.

*(Hunters use Steel the Resolve instead — see Chapter 2.)*

## The Beast Meter

Stress is what's happening to you tonight. The **Beast Meter** is what's happening to you *permanently*.

Where Stress rises and falls session to session, the Beast Meter only ever goes up. It runs from **0 to 10**, and it does not reset — not when you feed, not when you rest, not after a Time Skip. It is the running total of how much of yourself you've already lost.

At **Beast 10**, the thing you've been feeding takes over for good.

### THE RECKONING

At the end of every session, roll one **d6 for each point of Stress you currently have**.

**Every 1 rolled advances your Beast Meter by 1.**

That's the whole mechanic, and it happens once a session — never mid-scene. What matters is not how bad a single night got, but what you were still carrying when the night ended. A character who spent a session at Stress 5 and fed before dawn rolls the dice they finished on, not the ones they earned along the way.

It means Stress isn't just a countdown to a Time Skip — it's a tax. A character who ends sessions at 5 rolls five dice and will lose ground more often than not. A character who feeds before the night is out rolls one or two and might hold steady for months of play.

You cannot avoid the Reckoning. You can only ever be carrying less when it arrives.

*Note for Hunters:* you have no beast, but the Meter still runs. What advances is not possession — it's erosion. See the Hunter track below.

### What the Meter Does

Each step on the Beast Meter has a **narrative effect** specific to your Kind — something that has already happened to you and does not go away. These are listed in full below.

Four rungs also carry **mechanical weight**, and these are the same for every Kind:

- **Beast 3 — The First Crack.** Choose one stat and permanently reduce it by 1 (minimum 1). The beast has taken something you used to rely on.
- **Beast 6 — The Slipping.** Your Stress cap drops from 6 to 5. You break sooner than you used to.
- **Beast 9 — The Last Warning.** Your Stress cap drops to 4, and you can no longer reduce Stress below 1 by any means. There is always something awake in you now.
- **Beast 10 — The Beast Takes Control.** Your character is gone. No roll, no Surrender to the Years, no Long Hangover. They become an Epic-ranked NPC antagonist immediately, and you roll a Successor (see *Choosing Your Fate*). The difference between reaching 10 and capping Stress at 6 is that Stress is a crisis you come back from. This is not.

### Slowing the Meter

The Beast Meter cannot be reduced. It can only be *slowed*:

- Keeping Stress low is the only real defence. Every point of Stress you carry into a Reckoning is another die.
- A **strong hit** on Surrender to the Years lets you ignore the next Reckoning entirely — you clawed back something of yourself.
- Certain Rites, Relics, and Connections may let the GM waive a Reckoning once. These should be rare, costly, and never repeatable in the same way twice.

### The Beast Tracks

Each entry describes what has already happened at that step. The change is permanent and visible in the fiction — other characters notice, even if they can't name what's wrong.

#### VAMPIRE — The Thirst

1. Food and drink turn your stomach. You stop pretending at meals.
2. Your reflection unsettles you. You start covering mirrors without deciding to.
3. **(First Crack)** You stop feeling temperature. Warmth is something you notice only in other people.
4. You can no longer sleep in the presence of another person. Something in you refuses the vulnerability.
5. Your voice changes when you're hungry — a register mortals flinch from without knowing why.
6. **(Slipping)** You stop remembering the faces of people you've fed on. They blur together into one long meal.
7. You dream of the first person you killed, every day, and it's a fond dream.
8. Mortals in a room fall quiet when you enter. Nobody can say why. Children cry.
9. **(Last Warning)** You've stopped thinking of mortals as people at all. When you try, it's an effort of translation.
10. **(Control)** The thing wearing your face hunts openly now, and it does not remember your name.

#### GARGOYLE — The Stone

1. Your skin roughens permanently. Long sleeves stop being optional.
2. You wake standing, in postures you don't remember taking.
3. **(First Crack)** Fine motor control fails you. Small, human tasks — buttons, handwriting — become impossible.
4. You stop being able to speak above a low grind. Conversation becomes effortful for everyone.
5. Your territory feels like the only real place. Everywhere else is a kind of static.
6. **(Slipping)** You lose the instinct to hide. You have to consciously remember to conceal yourself.
7. Stone Slumber begins claiming you before dawn, unpredictably, without full sunlight.
8. You no longer recognize your own face in photographs from before.
9. **(Last Warning)** You've begun sleeping through nights as well as days. Waking is now the exception.
10. **(Control)** You do not wake again as yourself. Something ancient is using the shape you left behind.

#### WENDIGO — The Hollow

1. You stop tasting ordinary food. It registers as texture and nothing else.
2. Your teeth loosen and reset, sharper. You get used to it.
3. **(First Crack)** You lose weight that never comes back, no matter how much you consume.
4. The cold stops registering as cold. It registers as *comfortable*.
5. You start hearing your own voice in the mouths of others when you're hungry.
6. **(Slipping)** The Mask requires conscious effort now, even well-fed. You maintain it like holding your breath.
7. You can no longer remember what your original face looked like without a photograph.
8. You begin measuring people by what they'd provide. It happens before you can stop it.
9. **(Last Warning)** Your body no longer processes anything but human flesh. Everything else passes through you.
10. **(Control)** It walks out into the cold and it does not come back with your name.

#### WEREWOLF — The Turning

1. Your senses sharpen past comfort. Cities become overwhelming, constantly.
2. Your temper shortens permanently. People start managing you.
3. **(First Crack)** You lose the ability to sit still. Enclosed spaces become genuinely unbearable.
4. Your eyes stop changing back completely. People notice and look away.
5. You begin to think in terms of hierarchy in every room, without meaning to.
6. **(Slipping)** Partial transformation happens involuntarily under stress. Claws, jaw, posture.
7. You dream as the wolf and wake disappointed to be human.
8. You've started marking territory — doorways, thresholds — without conscious decision.
9. **(Last Warning)** You can no longer hold human shape for a full day. The change comes when it wants.
10. **(Control)** There is no man left to come back. There is only the thing that runs.

#### WITCH / WARLOCK — The Debt

1. Your handwriting changes. It isn't yours anymore, and it's more legible.
2. You stop needing to consult your notes. The workings are simply *in* you now.
3. **(First Crack)** Something has taken a memory as payment. A person, a year, a place — gone cleanly.
4. Your reflection lags behind you, very slightly, in the corner of your eye.
5. Mortals find your presence uncomfortable and can't articulate why.
6. **(Slipping)** You speak in your source's cadence when tired. Others hear it before you do.
7. Small workings happen around you without being cast — doors, flames, weather.
8. You've stopped being able to lie about what you serve. The truth surfaces.
9. **(Last Warning)** It answers questions put to you, occasionally, using your mouth.
10. **(Control)** The pact completes. What walks away has your knowledge, your face, and none of your intentions.

#### HUNTER — The Erosion

1. You stop being able to talk about your work with anyone outside it.
2. Sleep becomes unreliable. You start working the hours you can't sleep through.
3. **(First Crack)** You've lost a relationship you can't get back, and you're relieved.
4. You begin categorizing strangers by threat before you notice their faces.
5. Violence stops producing an adrenaline response. It's just a task now.
6. **(Slipping)** You can't remember why you started. You know the reason existed.
7. You start taking risks that read, to others, as wanting it to end.
8. You've begun making decisions other hunters won't defend, and you no longer explain them.
9. **(Last Warning)** You've done something you would once have hunted someone else for.
10. **(Control)** You don't become a monster. You become the thing that keeps killing after the reason is gone — and someone else will have to stop you.

#### GHOST — The Fading

1. Your edges blur in reflections and photographs. Nobody comments on it twice.
2. You lose your sense of duration. An hour and a week feel identical.
3. **(First Crack)** You forget a name that used to matter — permanently, with no way to recover it.
4. You can no longer remember the sound of your own living voice.
5. Places you haunted before begin rejecting you. The tether narrows.
6. **(Slipping)** You appear to strangers involuntarily, in moments of strong feeling.
7. You've forgotten how you died. The story you tell now is one you constructed.
8. You can't picture the living person you're tethered to unless they're in front of you.
9. **(Last Warning)** You've begun answering to a name that isn't yours, because it's the one people use.
10. **(Control)** What remains is a shape with a grievance and no memory of its cause. It haunts whatever's nearest.

## The Time Skip (Downtime)

When your Stress meter caps out at 6 — or at 5 or 4, once the Beast Meter has lowered your cap — your character completely loses control to the beast, or falls into a deep, agonizing despair (for Hunters). You are removed from play for months or years. When you return, the world has moved on.

A Time Skip is survivable. It costs you years, assets, and Connections, but you come back. Reaching Beast 10 is not survivable, and does not use this move.

### SURRENDER TO THE YEARS

When you lose control to your beast, or fall into a long despair, time marches on without you. The GM or system initiates a Time Skip. Roll +Heart to see what remains when you finally awaken or sober up.

- On a **strong hit**, you claw your way back before losing everything. You suffer a minor collateral consequence.
- On a **weak hit**, years pass in a blur of blood or ruin. You suffer severe collateral damage (e.g., a destroyed haven, a lost ally) and decrease the rank of one of your Connections.
- On a **miss**, you wake up to a nightmare of your own making. Your beast committed atrocities. You immediately take on the Doomed or Indebted burden, owing a massive debt to a dangerous faction, and face an immediate, deadly threat.

## Choosing Your Fate

After a Time Skip, the player has two options:

1. **The Long Hangover:** You keep playing the character. Your Stress is reset to zero, but you bear the permanent scars, debts, and enemies your beast accumulated during the missing years. **Your Beast Meter does not reset** — advance it by 1 for the years you lost.
2. **The Successor:** Your original character is too far gone. They become an Epic-ranked NPC antagonist monster roaming the city. You roll a new character — perhaps a childe, victim, or hunter connected to the original — and begin play with an **Epic-rank Vow** to deal with the monster you used to be.

**At Beast 10, you don't get the choice.** The Successor path is automatic, immediately, with no Surrender to the Years roll. The Long Hangover exists because a Time Skip is a crisis. Beast 10 is an ending.

# CHAPTER 4: VIOLENCE

Combat in this system is fast, lethal, and avoids tactical slogs. It uses the Rule of 3 to resolve skirmishes quickly, keeping the focus on the visceral horror of the fight.

## RESOLVE THE SKIRMISH (RULE OF 3)

When a fight breaks out, state your objective (e.g., escape the ambush, kill the alpha, protect a mortal) and make exactly three action rolls to fight, maneuver, or survive.

Track your **Advantage** tally across the three rolls:

- **Strong Hit:** You gain the upper hand (+1 Advantage).
- **Weak Hit:** A bloody exchange. No Advantage is gained, and you suffer a minor immediate cost (take a hit, lose gear).
- **Miss:** The enemy gains the upper hand (-1 Advantage), and you must Pay the Price.

After the third roll, check your final Advantage tally:

- **Advantage of +1 or more (Decisive Victory):** You seize the upper hand and achieve your objective with minimal narrative fallout. (You still carry costs from weak hits.)
- **Advantage of 0 (Success at a Cost):** A desperate stalemate. You achieve your objective, but the toll is heavy. Choose your price:
  - **Feed the Beast:** The exertion pushes you to the brink. Your Stress meter immediately spikes.
  - **Collateral Damage:** Your victory destroys something valuable, or an innocent is caught in the crossfire.
  - **The Mask Slips:** You leave behind undeniable evidence of your supernatural nature. Increase your Exposure by 1 (Chapter 13).
  - **Flesh and Bone:** You suffer a severe, lingering injury. Take a Condition of Dangerous rank (Chapter 12).
- **Advantage of -1 or less (Defeat):** You are overpowered. You fail to achieve your objective and must Face Defeat (Chapter 8), leaving you broken, captured, or at the mercy of your beast.

**Against Formidable, Extreme, or Epic enemies**, a Decisive Victory does not end them — mark progress on their track instead and get out with your objective. See Chapter 14.

# CHAPTER 5: CONNECTIONS

Connections represent the people, places, and factions still anchoring you to something beyond your own hunger. They are your leverage, your safety net, and — eventually — your liability.

## Connection Ranks

Each Connection is rated Weak, Steady, or Strong.

- **Strong:** This bond is a lifeline. When you call on it, add +1 to your roll, or take an automatic weak hit instead of rolling, once per session.
- **Steady:** This bond is reliable but ordinary. You can call on it for aid, information, or shelter, subject to a normal roll.
- **Weak:** This bond is frayed, strained, or nearly severed. Calling on it requires a roll +Heart to see if it holds at all; on a miss, the Connection breaks entirely and is removed from your sheet.

## Gaining and Losing Connections

- You may forge a new Connection (starting at Weak) whenever the fiction supports it — an ally earned, a debt repaid, a truce struck.
- A Connection's rank decreases whenever you use them carelessly, endanger them, or the fiction demonstrates the bond fraying (most commonly triggered by a weak hit or miss on Surrender to the Years).
- A Connection at Weak that decreases again is broken — remove it from your sheet. What that means in fiction is up to you and the GM: they could turn hostile, disappear, or simply be gone.

## BURN A CONNECTION

When you call on a Connection to save yourself from something you could not survive alone, you may Burn it instead of rolling.

- The Connection is immediately reduced by one rank (or removed, if already Weak).
- In exchange, you automatically achieve a strong hit on the roll that triggered this move.

This is the emergency lever — powerful, but it costs you a piece of your humanity's support structure every time you pull it.

# CHAPTER 6: BURDENS

Burdens are lasting marks against your character — debts, curses, or obligations incurred when things go badly wrong, most commonly from a miss on Surrender to the Years. Unlike Connections, Burdens don't fade with good behavior; they must be actively resolved.

## Doomed

Something is coming for you specifically — a rival, a curse, a prophecy, a debt collector who doesn't take no for an answer. While Doomed:

- The GM will periodically introduce complications tied to your doom, escalating over time if ignored.
- You gain +1 to any roll made in direct defiance of your doom (facing it head-on rather than running).
- Doomed is resolved by confronting its source directly, in a scene the GM frames as climactic — win or lose, the Burden is then removed.

## Indebted

You owe something significant to a dangerous faction — a life, a service, a stolen relic, an unpaid blood price. While Indebted:

- The faction may call in favors at the worst possible moment; refusing them increases your Stress immediately.
- You may reduce the debt by performing tasks the faction sets for you (GM's discretion on how many).
- Indebted is resolved when the debt is fully paid — by service, by an equivalent trade, or by the faction's destruction (which carries its own consequences).

**Stacking Burdens:** A character can carry multiple Burdens simultaneously. There's no mechanical cap, but GMs should treat a character drowning in unresolved Burdens as a strong signal that a Time Skip — or the Successor path — is coming whether the player chooses it or not.

# CHAPTER 7: CHARACTER CREATION

## Step Zero — The Cohort

Before anyone builds a character, the table answers one question together: **why are these creatures in the same room?**

A Vampire, a Hunter, and a Ghost have no natural reason to cooperate, and "we all met at a bar" collapses the first time someone's hunger becomes inconvenient. Your **Cohort** is the answer — the thing that holds the group together when the fiction is pulling it apart.

Choose one, or build your own:

**THE TRUCE** — *You are not friends. You are a solution to a shared problem.*
Something in this city threatens all of you regardless of Kind. You cooperate because the alternative is worse.
- **Shared asset:** One Haven, held in common, that nobody fully controls.
- **Pressure:** The moment the shared threat is gone, so is the reason.

**THE CELL** — *Someone organized you, and you said yes.*
A faction, an elder, or a Vigil handler assembles and directs you. You have resources and orders.
- **Shared asset:** One Companion — your handler or contact — available to all.
- **Pressure:** You don't choose your jobs, and you don't get told everything.

**THE FOUND FAMILY** — *You are what each other has left.*
Whatever you are, you've each lost everything else. This is the last thing standing.
- **Shared asset:** Each member holds a Connection to every other member at Steady rank.
- **Pressure:** You'd all cross a line for each other, and eventually one of you will ask.

**THE CREW** — *You do work. The work pays.*
Smuggling, cleanup, retrieval, protection — you sell a service to a market with no other suppliers.
- **Shared asset:** One Haven functioning as a base of operations, plus a reputation.
- **Pressure:** Your reputation is your income, and it's fragile.

**THE DEBT** — *You all owe the same thing to the same party.*
Whatever it was, it was significant, and it hasn't been paid.
- **Shared asset:** Everyone takes **Indebted** to the same holder, and each takes one benefit from the Step 6 menu.
- **Pressure:** Your creditor decides what you do next, and they know exactly where you all live.

**THE INVESTIGATION** — *You each found a piece of the same thing.*
Separately, you all pulled a thread. The threads led to the same place, and now you can't unsee it.
- **Shared asset:** One Relic, Archive, or body of evidence held collectively.
- **Pressure:** What you're uncovering is protected by people who protect things well.

### Cohort Rules

- **Everyone shares a Vow.** In addition to your personal Vow, the Cohort holds one shared Vow at Formidable rank or higher, tracked on a single progress track. All members mark it, and all members earn XP when it's fulfilled.
- **The shared asset is genuinely shared.** Any member may use it. Any member losing it loses it for everyone. It cannot be sold, hidden, or claimed by one character.
- **Cohorts break.** If the premise stops being true in the fiction, say so at the table and choose a new one, or let the group fracture. A Cohort collapsing is a good arc, not a failure.

### Step 1 — Choose Your Kind and Archetype

Pick one Kind: Vampire, Gargoyle, Wendigo, Werewolf, Witch/Warlock, Hunter, or Ghost. This decides your Stress Escalation triggers, your Recover Move, your Progression Triggers, and your Weakness (see Chapter 2).

Then pick one of that Kind's two Archetypes (see Kind Archetypes in Chapter 2). Your Archetype doesn't change what you are — it changes how you carry it, bending your Sate the Beast outcomes, your Stress triggers, or both. Everything else you build branches off these two choices.

### Step 2 — Assign Your Stats

Distribute these five values across your stats: **3, 2, 2, 1, 1**. Every stat must get one of these numbers, none repeated except the two 2s and two 1s.

- **Nerve** — quickness, guns, getting out of the way, keeping your head.
- **Heart** — resolve, compassion, reaching people, your hold on your humanity.
- **Bone** — strength, taking punishment, violence up close.
- **Shadow** — moving unseen, lying well, hunting from the dark.
- **Wits** — what you know, what you notice, thinking faster than the other side.

Put your highest stat where your character's Kind naturally excels — but you don't have to. A Vampire with high Heart and low Shadow is a compelling, unusual character precisely because it fights the type.

**The first-session swap.** After you've actually played the character once, you may move a single point between two stats — no explanation required. Character sheets always look different once they've hit a table, and one session of play tells you more about what you built than an hour of planning did.

### Step 3 — Set Starting Stress

All characters begin play at **Stress 2** and **Beast 0**, regardless of Kind. You're not starting in crisis, but you're not stable either — the hunger (or doubt, for Hunters) is already present, and the long slide hasn't begun.

### Step 4 — Choose Your Vow

Every character begins with a **Vow**: a driving goal that gives your character a reason to act rather than just react. A Vow can be as personal as protecting a specific person, or as large as unraveling a citywide conspiracy. Rate your Vow's difficulty:

- **Troublesome** — achievable in a session or two.
- **Dangerous** — a real arc, several sessions long.
- **Formidable** — a campaign-spanning goal.
- **Extreme** — the kind of Vow that could end the character's story, one way or another.

Your Vow isn't fixed forever — it can be fulfilled, abandoned, or replaced as the story moves, but you always have one active. Ranks, progress tracks, and the Fulfil Your Vow move are in Chapter 11.

### Step 5 — Establish Starting Connections

Choose **two Connections** to start play with. For each, decide:

- Who or what they are (a person, a faction, a place).
- Their starting rank: one **Steady** and one **Weak**, or two **Weak** — your choice.
- One sentence describing why this bond still matters to your character, despite what they are now.

These are the people and things your character hasn't let go of yet. Play will test every one of them.

### Step 6 — Take on a Starting Burden (Optional)

Most characters begin play burden-free. If you want a character with more baggage from the start — someone already deep into their story rather than at its beginning — you may choose to start with one Burden (Doomed or Indebted).

**You may take at most one Burden at creation.** In exchange, choose exactly one of the following:

- Raise one starting Connection by one rank (Weak → Steady, or Steady → Strong)
- Take a second ability on any one of your starting assets
- Take one additional Connection at Weak rank
- Begin play at Stress 1 instead of 2

Tell the GM what the Burden is and who holds it. A starting Burden should point at something the campaign can actually use — a faction in the city, a rival with a name, a debt with terms. "Something is coming for me eventually" is not a Burden; it's a mood.

### Step 7 — Choose a Power, a Path, Gear, and Resources

Choose one **Power** from your Kind's list (Chapter 9) and take its first ability, free.

Choose one **Path** (Chapter 10) and take its first ability, free. Your Path is what you were before this — everyone has a before, and it still shapes what you're good at.

Then list 2–3 pieces of gear, resources, or assets that fit your Kind and background: a car, a weapon, a safehouse key, a grimoire, a badge, a phone full of contacts. These aren't tracked with detailed inventory rules — they exist to justify adds and fictional positioning when the moment calls for them ("I do have lockpicks for exactly this").

### Step 8 — Name and Background

Give your character a name, and answer these three questions to ground them before play begins:

1. **What were you before?** (Or, for Ghosts: what were you in life?)
2. **What was the moment you knew you weren't human anymore?**
3. **Who knows what you really are — and how are they handling it?**

## Example Builds

Two worked characters, start to finish, showing how the pieces fit together.

### Example One: Ilse Brandt, the Graverobber

**Step 1 — Kind and Archetype.** Wendigo, **Graverobber**. She refuses to kill. That refusal is the whole character.

*What this locks in:* Stress escalates from extreme cold, witnessing mortal vulnerability, and starvation. Recover via Sate the Beast (consuming human flesh). Her Weakness — the hunger only grows — means her equilibrium is temporary by design. As a Graverobber, scavenged flesh feeds her properly (2 Stress on a strong hit), but The Slipping Mask advances faster between feedings.

**Step 2 — Stats.** Shadow 3, Wits 2, Heart 2, Bone 1, Nerve 1.

She's a scavenger, not a fighter. Shadow 3 means she takes covert feeding with a real chance of success, which is exactly how a Graverobber survives. Bone 1 is a deliberate weakness — if a fight starts, she's in trouble.

**Step 3 — Stress.** Starts at 2, as everyone does.

**Step 4 — Vow.** *Find out who else is taking bodies from the city morgues, before they get me blamed for it.* **Dangerous** — a real arc, several sessions.

**Step 5 — Connections.**
- **Marek, night-shift morgue attendant** — Steady. He takes her money and doesn't ask. He thinks she's selling to medical schools.
- **Her sister Anneke** — Weak. Anneke thinks Ilse moved abroad six years ago. They text on birthdays.

**Step 6 — Burden.** None. She's stable, for now. That's the story to ruin.

**Step 7 — Power, Path, Gear.**
- **Power: The Cold Larder** (Graverobber), first ability — she maintains a hidden supply and always has a feed available unless the fiction takes it from her.
- **Path: Doctor**, first ability — she can treat serious mundane injury with what's on hand, +1 to stabilize someone. She was a physician before. It's also how she knows morgues.
- **Gear:** a refrigerated van with a broken GPS unit, a set of surgical tools, a phone with exactly four numbers in it.

**Step 8 — Name and background.** Ilse Brandt.
1. *What were you before?* An emergency physician in Hamburg.
2. *When did you know you weren't human?* Four days trapped after an avalanche on a hiking trip, with two other survivors. Only she walked out.
3. *Who knows what you really are?* Nobody living. Marek suspects something, but has decided the money is worth not knowing.

**What this build actually does at the table:** Shadow 3 plus The Cold Larder means Ilse can usually avoid hunting — which is the point of the Graverobber. The pressure doesn't come from her failing rolls; it comes from her supply being threatened. Take away the morgue, and every session becomes about how long she can hold the line. Her Doctor path gives her a reason to be near bodies and a reason people trust her. Bone 1 means she cannot solve a problem with violence, ever.

### Example Two: Samuel Okonjo, the Pragmatist

**Step 1 — Kind and Archetype.** Hunter, **Pragmatist**. He's worked with monsters before and it went fine, mostly.

*What this locks in:* No beast, no Sate the Beast. He recovers via **Steel the Resolve** (+Heart or +Wits). His Stress comes from moral erosion. As a Pragmatist, working with monsters doesn't trigger Stress on its own — only genuine betrayal does — and he can call on a monstrous Connection through Steel the Resolve's +Heart option.

**Step 2 — Stats.** Wits 3, Heart 2, Nerve 2, Shadow 1, Bone 1.

Wits 3 makes him an investigator first and a fighter never. Heart 2 supports his recovery move and his alliances. Two 1s mean he genuinely cannot brawl or sneak — he has to think or talk his way through.

**Step 3 — Stress.** 2.

**Step 4 — Vow.** *Keep the truce in this city from collapsing, whatever the Vigil thinks about it.* **Formidable** — campaign-spanning.

**Step 5 — Connections.**
- **Teodora, a Vampire who runs a bar as neutral ground** — Weak. They've helped each other twice. Neither has decided if they're friends.
- **His old Vigil cell** — Weak. They think he's compromised. They're not entirely wrong.

**Step 6 — Burden.** He takes **Indebted** at creation, and chooses the Connection upgrade from the Step 6 menu. He owes a favor to a Witch who kept him alive after a hunt went wrong, and she hasn't called it in yet. Teodora upgrades from Weak to **Steady**.

**Step 7 — Power, Path, Gear.**
- **Power: Monster Lore** (Hunter), first ability — +1 to Gather Information about supernatural creatures.
- **Path: Journalist**, first ability — +2 to Gather Information through interview or conversation.
- **Gear:** a car that's seen better decades, a locked case he doesn't open often, a notebook nobody else could read.

**Step 8 — Name and background.** Samuel Okonjo.
1. *What were you before?* A local crime reporter.
2. *When did you know?* A story about missing people that turned out to be exactly what it looked like.
3. *Who knows what you are?* Everyone he works with. That's the problem — hunters and monsters both know, and neither fully trusts him for it.

**What this build actually does at the table:** Samuel stacks Wits 3 with two separate Gather Information bonuses. The +2 cap means those don't combine past +2 — his ceiling is a reliable, not automatic, 5 before the die. He's the best investigator at any table he sits at without becoming immune to failure. He is also nearly helpless in a Skirmish, which forces him toward negotiation — exactly what the Pragmatist archetype rewards. When his assets clearly over-cover a question, the GM should hand him the answer rather than roll (see *Step 2 — Gather your adds*, Chapter 1). The Indebted burden is a loaded gun the GM can fire at any moment, and taking it bought him the one Connection that makes his Vow possible.

# CHAPTER 8: MOVES REFERENCE

A quick-lookup index of every move in this book, plus the generic moves that cover anything the Kind-specific and Violence moves don't. Full text and exceptions live in the chapter listed — this chapter is for finding the right move fast at the table.

## Generic Moves

These moves cover risky action outside of feeding, fighting, and time skips. If a situation doesn't clearly trigger a Kind-specific or Violence move, one of these almost always will.

Each of these moves has a d6 miss oracle in Chapter 16.

### FACE DANGER

When you attempt something risky or uncertain that isn't covered by another move — sneaking past a guard, climbing somewhere you shouldn't, holding a door shut against something trying to get in — roll +the stat that best fits your approach.

- **Strong hit:** You do it, cleanly.
- **Weak hit:** You do it, but at a cost — you're spotted, you're hurt, you're slower than you needed to be.
- **Miss:** Pay the Price.

### SECURE AN ADVANTAGE

When you spend time and effort setting up for what's coming — scouting a location, preparing a weapon, positioning yourself before a confrontation — roll +the stat that fits your preparation.

- **Strong hit:** Take +2 to add to your next roll related to this setup, or +1 to two separate rolls.
- **Weak hit:** Take +1 to add to your next related roll, but something about the setup is imperfect or incomplete.
- **Miss:** Your preparation backfires or is wasted. Pay the Price.

### GATHER INFORMATION

When you investigate a person, place, or situation — interrogating a contact, digging through records, reading a scene for clues — roll +Wits.

- **Strong hit:** You learn something true and useful. Ask the GM a question about the subject; they answer honestly.
- **Weak hit:** You learn something, but it's incomplete, outdated, or comes with a complication attached.
- **Miss:** You learn something you didn't want to know, or your searching draws unwanted attention. Pay the Price.

### COMPEL

When you push someone — mortal or otherwise — to do, give up, or reveal something they wouldn't offer freely, through threat, manipulation, or force of will, roll +Heart (personal conviction) or +Shadow (manipulation and threat).

- **Strong hit:** They comply, mostly on your terms.
- **Weak hit:** They comply, but push back — they want something in return, or comply only partially.
- **Miss:** They refuse outright, and now know what you were after. Pay the Price.

### SLIP AWAY

When you attempt to escape a pursuer, a bad situation, or a scene before it turns worse, roll +Nerve.

- **Strong hit:** You get clear, no complications.
- **Weak hit:** You get clear, but leave something behind — evidence, an ally, your composure.
- **Miss:** You don't get away. Pay the Price.

### AID ANOTHER

When you actively help someone else with what they're attempting — physically, socially, or by covering the thing they can't — say how, and roll +the stat that fits your help.

- **Strong hit:** They add +2 to their roll.
- **Weak hit:** They add +1, and you're exposed to the same danger they are — whatever cost they take, you share it.
- **Miss:** Your help makes it worse. They roll at −1, and you Pay the Price.

You must be present and doing something specific. "I encourage them" is not aid. Aid counts toward the +2 cap on adds like anything else.

### UNDERTAKE A JOURNEY

When you travel somewhere dangerous, distant, or hard to reach — across a city under Heat, along the Hollow Roads, out of the country with something you shouldn't have — set the journey's rank, then roll +Wits (navigation and planning) or +Nerve (speed and nerve) for each leg.

- **Strong hit:** Mark progress. The leg passes without incident.
- **Weak hit:** Mark progress, but the journey costs you — time, supplies, a Condition, or attention you didn't want.
- **Miss:** No progress. You're delayed, lost, intercepted, or somewhere worse than where you started. Pay the Price.

When you have enough progress to try, roll to arrive exactly as you would Fulfil Your Vow (Chapter 11). A strong hit means you arrive prepared and unnoticed; a weak hit means you arrive, but something followed, broke, or was left behind; a miss means you arrive to find the situation has changed against you.

### FACE DEFEAT

When a Skirmish or other conflict ends in your defeat, roll +Heart to determine what you're left with.

- **Strong hit:** You're beaten, but not broken. You keep your secrets, your gear, and your standing — only your pride and your body take the hit.
- **Weak hit:** You survive, but you lose something in the process — a piece of gear, a Connection's trust, a secret exposed.
- **Miss:** Defeat costs you dearly. Take on a Burden (Doomed or Indebted, GM's choice given the fiction), in addition to whatever narrative consequence the GM introduces.

# CHAPTER 9: POWERS

## How Powers Work

Powers are the supernatural capabilities that separate you from the mortals around you. Each Power is an asset card with three abilities, listed in order.

**Taking a Power** costs 2 XP and grants you its first ability. **Each additional ability** on that Power costs 3 XP, and must be bought in order — you can't take the third ability without the second.

**Two lists are open to you.** Every Kind has six **Kind Powers**, available to anyone of that Kind. Each Archetype adds two **Archetype Powers** on top, available only to characters of that Archetype. If you later change Archetype through play, you keep any Archetype Powers you've already bought — but they may work differently, and the GM should say how.

**At character creation**, you begin with one Power from either list and its first ability, free.

**Using a Power** doesn't automatically require a roll. Most Powers either modify an existing move, grant a new option, or simply describe something you can now do. When a Power says "roll," roll. When it doesn't, it just works — see *Not Everything Is a Move* in Chapter 1.

**Powers and Stress.** Several Powers have a Stress cost. This is deliberate: the more you lean on what you are, the more the Beast asserts itself. A Power that costs Stress can always be used, but the meter doesn't care about your reasons.

# CHAPTER 10: ASSETS

## How Assets Work

Powers (Chapter 9) are what your Kind makes possible. Assets are everything else that makes you *you* — your history, your ground, the people beside you, the objects you'd die before losing, and the procedures you've learned to perform.

Every asset works like a Power: three abilities, bought in order.

- **Taking an asset** costs 2 XP and grants its first ability.
- **Each further ability** costs 3 XP and must be bought in sequence.

**At character creation**, in addition to your free starting Power, choose **one Path** and take its first ability free. Paths are what you were before, and everyone has a before.

**Asset types:**

| Type | What It Represents | Gated By |
|---|---|---|
| **Paths** | Your history, training, and mundane competence | Nothing — open to all |
| **Havens** | Ground you hold and can retreat to | Nothing — open to all |
| **Companions** | A person or creature present with you in scenes | Nothing — open to all |
| **Relics** | Objects with weight, history, or power | Nothing — open to all |
| **Rites** | Learned procedures requiring preparation | Some require a Kind |
| **Powers** | Innate supernatural capability | Kind and Archetype (Ch. 9) |

**Companions vs. Connections.** A Connection (Chapter 5) is someone you *call on* — they exist off-screen and can be burned for an automatic strong hit. A Companion is *with you*, acts in scenes, and can be hurt, taken, or turned. A person can be both, but a Companion who is also a Connection is doubly exposed.

**Relics and Weaknesses.** Some Kinds already have a Weakness tied to an object — the Witch's anchor, the Ghost's tether. If you take a Relic that fills that role, it is genuinely double-edged: everything it grants you is also everything an enemy can take away. Play that honestly.

**Rites take time.** Unlike Powers, a Rite is never instantaneous. Every Rite requires preparation, materials, or ceremony that the fiction must accommodate. If there's no time to perform it, there's no Rite.

## Assets and the Time Skip

Assets do not survive a Time Skip cleanly. On **Surrender to the Years**:

- **Strong hit:** All assets remain. You may buy one new ability on any asset free of XP cost.
- **Weak hit:** Lose one asset entirely, or reduce two assets by one ability each. Player's choice.
- **Miss:** Lose two assets entirely, GM's choice, and one surviving asset is corrupted — its abilities still function, but the fiction around them has turned against you. A Haven now belongs to someone else. A Companion resents you. A Relic has been used for something while you were gone.

Havens, Companions, and Relics are the assets the world can take. That's the point of them.

# CHAPTER 11: VOWS, RANKS, AND PROGRESSION

## Ranks

Everything difficult in Blood & Bone has a **rank**. Vows, enemies, investigations, journeys, and long projects all use the same five-step ladder, and rank means the same thing everywhere: how much work it takes to get through.

| Rank | What It Means | Progress Per Mark |
|---|---|---|
| **Troublesome** | A night's problem. A single obstacle. | 3 boxes |
| **Dangerous** | A real arc. Several sessions. | 2 boxes |
| **Formidable** | A campaign thread. Months of play. | 1 box |
| **Extreme** | The kind of thing that defines a chronicle. | 2 ticks |
| **Epic** | Almost beyond you. Elders, ancient pacts, city-shaping forces. | 1 tick |

## Progress Tracks

A progress track is **ten boxes**, each divided into **four ticks** — forty ticks total.

When you make meaningful progress toward a ranked objective, you **mark progress**: fill in the amount listed above for that rank. Four ticks fill a box.

You mark progress when the fiction earns it — not on a schedule. Defeating a lieutenant, uncovering a crucial name, surviving a journey leg, completing a ritual step. The GM decides when progress is earned; the rank decides how much.

**Marking progress on a miss.** You never mark progress on a miss. A miss means the obstacle held.

## FULFIL YOUR VOW

When you believe you've done everything the Vow required and move to complete it, don't make an action roll. Instead:

1. Count your **filled boxes** — ticks in a partially filled box don't count.
2. That total is your **progress score**, from 0 to 10.
3. Roll the two Challenge Dice and compare, exactly as you would an Action Score.

- **Strong hit:** The Vow is fulfilled. Take XP per the table below.
- **Weak hit:** The Vow is fulfilled, but not cleanly — something you wanted is lost, or the resolution creates a new problem. Take XP as normal, minus 1 (minimum 1).
- **Miss:** The Vow is not fulfilled. Something you believed was wrong. Either abandon the Vow, or the GM reveals what remains and you continue with your progress reduced to half its current boxes, rounded down.

You may attempt to Fulfil Your Vow at any time. Attempting it at two boxes is a legitimate, desperate choice, and the dice will treat it accordingly.

## Forsaking a Vow

You may abandon a Vow at any point. Clear its track, and take **1 Stress** — it costs something to stop caring about a thing you swore to. You do not gain XP.

A Vow forsaken is often more interesting than one fulfilled. The people who were counting on it are still out there.

## Downtime

Not every gap between sessions is a Time Skip. Most of them are just a quiet week where nothing is hunting you.

Between sessions, each character may take **two Downtime actions** from the list below. Downtime is narrated, not played out — say what you did, resolve it, move on.

**MEND** — Mark progress on one Condition per its rank, or roll to Clear a Condition if you have progress to spend.

**LAY LOW** — Attempt Clean Up (Chapter 13) to reduce Exposure. Doing so in downtime means it costs you visibility rather than risk: you were gone, and people noticed you were gone.

**REPAIR A BOND** — Spend time with one Connection. Roll +Heart: on a strong hit, raise its rank by one. On a weak hit, it holds where it is and you learn something they've been hiding. On a miss, whatever's been wrong between you surfaces badly.

**WORK THE VOW** — Mark progress on one Vow per its rank, if the fiction supports off-screen effort. You must be able to say specifically what you did.

**FORTIFY** — Improve a Haven, prepare a Rite's materials, or set up something for what's coming. Take +1 to your first roll next session relating to that preparation.

**FEED CAREFULLY** — Sate the Beast (or Steel the Resolve) with no time pressure and no witnesses. Roll as normal, but treat a miss as a weak hit. You had the luxury of being careful.

**REACH OUT** — Contact a faction, ask a favour, or open a negotiation. The GM tells you what response is waiting at the start of next session.

### Downtime and the Reckoning

The Reckoning happens at the end of the session, **before** downtime. You cannot use downtime to reduce Stress and dodge the dice — the tax is collected on the night you finished, not the week that followed.

This is deliberate. Downtime is for repairing what the session cost you. It is not a way out of what the session did to you.

## The XP Economy

**XP comes from three places:**

| Source | Award |
|---|---|
| **Fulfilling a Vow** | Troublesome 1 · Dangerous 2 · Formidable 3 · Extreme 4 · Epic 5 |
| **Kind Progression Triggers** (Ch. 2) | 1, 3, or 5 as listed |
| **Archetype XP / Alternate XP** (Ch. 2) | 1, 3, or 5 as listed |

**Expected yield.** A typical session should produce **3–6 XP** per character. If your table is consistently earning more than 8, you're running both Archetype and Alternate XP sets and hitting most of them — either drop to one set, or accept a faster, more powerful game. If you're earning less than 2, triggers aren't coming up in play, and the GM should be framing scenes that put them in reach.

**Each trigger pays once per session**, however many times you technically satisfy it. Feeding undetected four times in a night is 1 XP, not 4.

## Spending XP

| Purchase | Cost |
|---|---|
| **New asset** (Power, Path, Haven, Companion, Relic, Rite) — grants first ability | 2 XP |
| **Next ability** on an asset you already have | 3 XP |
| **Raise a stat by 1** (maximum 4) | 10 XP |
| **Raise a Connection by one rank** | 3 XP |

**Stats above 3.** A stat may be raised to 4 through play, but never at creation, and never higher. A 4 in any stat marks a character as genuinely exceptional — most creatures in the setting, including most elders, sit at 3.

**You cannot buy off the Beast Meter.** No amount of XP touches it. That's the point of it.

# CHAPTER 12: HARM AND CONDITIONS

## No Hit Points

Blood & Bone doesn't track wounds numerically. A Skirmish is three rolls and an outcome, not an exchange of damage. What lasts after a fight isn't a number — it's a **Condition**.

## Conditions

A Condition is a lasting problem: an injury, an impairment, a state you're stuck in. Conditions come from weak hits and misses, from Skirmish costs like *Flesh and Bone*, from Paying the Price, and from the fiction generally.

**Every Condition applies −1 to rolls it plausibly affects.** The GM and player agree on scope when it's taken — a shattered hand affects Bone and fine work, not conversation.

**You can carry three Conditions.** Taking a fourth means you're **Broken** (below).

Conditions have a **rank**, which determines how hard they are to clear:

| Condition Severity | Rank | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Minor | Troublesome | Cracked ribs, deep laceration, badly sprained |
| Serious | Dangerous | Broken limb, severe burns, concussion |
| Grave | Formidable | Destroyed hand, punctured lung, silver wound |
| Permanent | — | Cannot be cleared; becomes part of the character |

## CLEAR A CONDITION

When you take real time and appropriate care to recover — rest in a Haven, treatment from a Companion or Path, or your Kind's natural healing — mark progress on the Condition's track per its rank. When you have progress to spend, roll to clear it exactly as you would Fulfil Your Vow.

- **Strong hit:** The Condition is cleared entirely.
- **Weak hit:** The Condition is cleared, but downgrade it to a lesser Condition rather than removing it, or it leaves a permanent mark that is cosmetic only.
- **Miss:** It hasn't healed, and something has gone wrong — infection, a badly-set bone, something worse. The GM may raise its rank.

**Supernatural healing.** Kinds and Powers that heal rapidly (Regeneration, The Old Blood) clear Troublesome and Dangerous Conditions automatically between sessions, provided the character has fed. They do not clear Grave Conditions, and they never clear silver wounds for Werewolves or sunlight damage for Vampires — those follow the mundane track.

**Hunters have no shortcut.** They clear Conditions through time, medicine, and other people, exactly as the Hunter Weakness in Chapter 2 says.

## BROKEN

When you take a fourth Condition, you are **Broken**. You are not dead — death is rare in this game and usually a choice — but you are out of the fight, out of the scene, and at the mercy of whoever is standing over you.

While Broken, you cannot take any action but speaking. To recover, someone must get you somewhere safe, and you must clear at least one Condition. Waking up Broken somewhere you don't recognize is one of the most common ways a chronicle turns.

## Death

A character dies only when the player agrees, or when the fiction leaves absolutely no other reading. If a character would die and the player isn't ready, the GM should reach for Broken, a Time Skip, a Burden, or capture instead. This game has better endings than death available — the Successor path exists precisely so that losing a character can be a beginning.

# CHAPTER 13: EXPOSURE AND THE MASQUERADE

## Why This Exists

Half the costs in this book — *The Mask Slips*, a witness left alive, a body found, a livestream that got out — reference exposure without tracking it. Exposure gives those costs memory. Without it, breaking the masquerade is a mood; with it, it's a countdown.

## The Exposure Meter

Each character has an **Exposure** meter running from **0 to 6**, tracking how much evidence of *you specifically* exists in the world.

**Exposure increases by 1 when:**
- You take *The Mask Slips* as a Skirmish cost
- A mortal witnesses something they cannot explain and survives
- You leave physical evidence — a body, footage, a scene that doesn't read as human
- A Power is used openly in front of people who will talk

**Exposure thresholds:**

- **1–2 — Rumour.** Stories circulate. Nobody credible believes them. No mechanical effect.
- **3 — Attention.** Someone is actively looking. The GM introduces an investigator, journalist, hunter, or rival who now has your scent. −1 to Slip Away in your home city.
- **4 — Pressure.** Your face or description is in a file somewhere. Havens are compromised: choose one, and it's being watched. Connections start distancing themselves — one drops a rank.
- **5 — Hunted.** Something is actively hunting you, and it is competent. You cannot recover Stress in your home city without first dealing with pursuit.
- **6 — Burned.** You are known. Your own Kind's political structures move against you for the threat you represent. You must leave the city, or the chronicle becomes about surviving it. Take the **Doomed** Burden immediately.

## CLEAN UP

When you take deliberate action to destroy evidence, discredit a witness, or bury a story, roll +Shadow (covert work) or +Wits (misdirection and forgery).

- **Strong hit:** Reduce your Exposure by 2.
- **Weak hit:** Reduce your Exposure by 1, and doing so cost you something — a favour owed, a Connection compromised, evidence that now exists somewhere else.
- **Miss:** The cleanup made it worse. Increase Exposure by 1 and Pay the Price.

You may attempt Clean Up once per session. It is not a substitute for not being seen.

## City Heat

The GM also tracks a single **Heat** meter for the city itself, from 0 to 6, rising when *anyone* — player or NPC — breaks the masquerade badly. Heat represents mortal institutional attention: police task forces, federal interest, press investigations, a public that's started noticing.

- **Heat 1–2:** Business as usual. Isolated incidents, dismissed.
- **Heat 3:** Mortal authorities have a pattern. Investigations become a recurring obstacle.
- **Heat 4:** A dedicated unit exists. Supernatural factions start enforcing the Concord violently to bring Heat down.
- **Heat 5:** Public awareness. Amateur hunters, viral footage, panic. Every Kind in the city is now in danger from ordinary people.
- **Heat 6:** The city is lost. Mass exodus of the supernatural, or open conflict. The chronicle changes permanently.

Heat falls by 1 at the end of any session where no masquerade breach occurred and someone actively worked to suppress attention. It is meant to be a shared problem — the fastest way to unite rival factions is a Heat of 4.

# CHAPTER 14: RUNNING THE GAME

## Antagonists Don't Have Sheets

NPCs in Blood & Bone don't have stats, and they never roll. When a player acts against an enemy, the *player* rolls; the enemy's rank determines how hard that is and how long it takes.

An antagonist needs exactly three things:

1. **A rank** (Troublesome through Epic)
2. **A want** — what they're actually trying to get
3. **One or two things they can do that the players can't**

That's the whole stat block.

## Antagonist Ranks

| Rank | Who That Is | How It Plays |
|---|---|---|
| **Troublesome** | A mortal thug, a fledgling, a panicking witness | Resolved in a scene. A single roll often does it. |
| **Dangerous** | An experienced hunter, an established Kind, a pack lieutenant | A Skirmish, or a short investigation. Genuine threat to one character. |
| **Formidable** | A court elder, an alpha, a coven leader | Cannot be beaten in one fight. Requires a plan, a progress track, allies. |
| **Extreme** | A centuries-old power, a Vigil strike cell, a faction head | The spine of a chronicle arc. Attacking directly is usually suicide. |
| **Epic** | An ancient, a city-shaping force, a former PC lost to the Beast | Cannot be defeated conventionally at all. Must be outmanoeuvred, bargained with, or unmade. |

**Rank sets the progress track**, so a Formidable enemy takes 1 box per meaningful victory — ten real wins to fill the track. That's what makes an elder feel like an elder without a single stat.

## Running a Skirmish Against a Ranked Enemy

The Rule of 3 resolves the immediate fight. Against **Troublesome** and **Dangerous** enemies, a Decisive Victory ends them.

Against **Formidable and above, a Decisive Victory does not.** It marks progress on their track and gets you out alive with your objective. The enemy withdraws, escalates, or reveals more of what they are. You cannot punch through rank — you grind it down, or you find another way entirely.

## The Six Threats

When you need an antagonist and don't have one, pick a shape:

1. **The Rival** — wants what you want, and is closer to getting it.
2. **The Hunter** — wants you specifically, and is patient about it.
3. **The Institution** — doesn't know you exist, and will crush you procedurally if you become a problem.
4. **The Hungry Thing** — has no plan, only appetite, and is making the city dangerous for everyone.
5. **The Creditor** — is owed something, by you or by someone you care about.
6. **The Mirror** — is what you'll become if you keep going, and knows it.

## GM Principles, Expanded

Chapter 1 lists five. Here they are with teeth:

**Make the world indifferent, not hostile.** The city doesn't hate the characters. It simply has its own business, and they're in the way of some of it. This is scarier than malice.

**Every faction wants something specific.** "The Undersett opposes you" is weak. "The Undersett needs the Heat down before the audit, and you're the reason it's up" is playable.

**Name the cost before the roll where you can.** If a player knows what a miss might cost, the roll carries weight. If you invent the cost afterward, it can feel arbitrary even when it isn't.

**Let Stress and the Beast Meter do the horror.** You do not need to punish players. The system already has a slow, permanent, unavoidable engine of loss running underneath every session. Trust it.

**Ask what the Beast wants.** When a player is deciding, ask them what their hunger is arguing for. Make them say it out loud. That single question does more character work than any mechanic in this book.

**Follow the fiction, not the plan.** If the players burn down the thing you prepared, the thing that replaces it is more interesting because they made it happen.

## Framing Scenes

Start scenes late and end them early. A scene begins when something is already in motion and ends the moment its outcome is clear. You do not need to play out the drive there.

Every scene should have at least one of: a decision with a cost, information the players want, or pressure on a Connection, Haven, or Vow.

If a scene has none of those, skip it and describe what happened.

## Pacing Stress

The GM's most important lever is *when the hunger becomes inconvenient*. A Vampire at Stress 4 who needs to attend a Salon function is a whole session. Escalate Stress by the Kind's stated triggers, but choose your moments — the meter is at its best when it forces a choice between what the character wants and what they need.

# CHAPTER 15: SOLO AND CO-OP PLAY

Blood & Bone runs without a GM. Solo, you play one character and let the oracles answer for the world. Co-op, two or more players share the GM's authority and ask the oracles when nobody at the table should get to decide.

## The Core Loop, GM-less

1. **Frame the scene.** State where you are and what you want.
2. **Act.** Describe what your character does.
3. **Roll if it's uncertain** — the same rules apply, including *Not Everything Is a Move*.
4. **Interpret.** On a strong hit, you decide what happens. On a weak hit or miss, ask an oracle.
5. **Record consequences.** Stress, Exposure, progress, Conditions.

The discipline is in step 4: when things go well, you narrate. When they go badly, you *ask* rather than decide.

## THE YES/NO ORACLE

When you need a closed question answered — is anyone here? does she believe me? is the door locked? — decide how likely it is, then roll a d10.

| Likelihood | Yes on |
|---|---|
| Almost certain | 1–9 |
| Likely | 1–7 |
| Even odds | 1–5 |
| Unlikely | 1–3 |
| Almost impossible | 1 |

**Doubles rule:** roll a second d10 alongside. If both dice show the same number, the answer is yes *and* something unexpected is attached — an "and" or a "but" that complicates the situation. This is where solo play generates surprises you didn't plant.

## THE COMPLICATION ORACLE

When you miss and need to Pay the Price but don't know how, roll d10:

1. A Connection is endangered or compromised.
2. A witness sees something they shouldn't. Exposure +1.
3. Your Stress escalates immediately by 1.
4. You take a Condition.
5. Something you need is lost, broken, or taken.
6. An enemy learns where you are, or who you are.
7. A Haven, Companion, or Relic is threatened.
8. The situation escalates — more enemies, less time, worse ground.
9. You succeed, but at a moral cost you'll carry.
10. Something from your past resurfaces at the worst moment.

## THE SCENE ORACLE

When you don't know what a new scene contains, roll d10:

1. Nothing. The quiet is real, and you should use it.
2. Someone is already here, and they were waiting.
3. Evidence of something that happened recently.
4. A mortal in the wrong place.
5. A rival's work, unmistakably.
6. An opportunity you didn't plan for.
7. A trap, or something that functions as one.
8. Someone you know, in a context that makes no sense.
9. The aftermath of violence.
10. Something wrong that you can't name yet.

## THE ORACLE OF WHAT THEY WANT

When you don't know an NPC's motive, roll d10:

1. Money, or its equivalent.
2. Safety — theirs or someone else's.
3. Information you have.
4. Revenge, and they've waited.
5. Status within their own faction.
6. To be left alone, completely.
7. Someone found — alive or otherwise.
8. Something destroyed.
9. A debt paid, in either direction.
10. Something you'd never guess. Roll twice and combine.

## Solo Vows

Solo play works best with **two Vows running**: one Troublesome or Dangerous for immediate direction, one Formidable or higher for the long arc. When you don't know what to do next, act on the smaller one.

## Co-op Authority

In co-op, rotate a **Director** role each session. The Director frames scenes, plays NPCs, and calls for rolls — but their own character is subject to the oracles like anyone's, and any player may call for an oracle roll instead of a Director ruling.

The Director never invents costs for their own character's misses. Those always go to the Complication Oracle.

# CHAPTER 16: PAYING THE PRICE

## Why These Tables Exist

A miss should never mean "nothing happens" or "you fail, try again." It means the situation moved, and it moved against you. But inventing a fresh consequence at the table, twelve times a session, is genuinely hard — and the pressure to be interesting tends to produce either the same cost every time or something arbitrary.

These oracles fix that. Every move in the book has a **d6 miss table** specific to what that move was trying to do. Roll it when you don't already know what happens, or roll it and use it as a prompt rather than an instruction.

**How to use them:**

- The GM, or the player in solo play, rolls d6 on the table for the move that missed.
- Take the result and make it *specific to this fiction*. "A Connection is compromised" is a prompt; naming which one, and how, is the actual answer.
- If the result doesn't fit what's happening, roll again or pick. These tables serve the table, not the reverse.
- On a **matched miss** (both Challenge Dice showing the same number), roll twice and apply both, or apply one result at double severity.

If a move has no table, or the situation is stranger than the table covers, use the **Complication Oracle** in Chapter 15.

## Generic Moves

### FACE DANGER — Miss
1. You're hurt. Take a Troublesome Condition.
2. You're caught, cornered, or held — you're not going anywhere for now.
3. You succeed at the wrong thing. The obstacle is passed, but you've made a worse one.
4. Someone sees you do something you can't explain. Exposure +1.
5. You lose something in the attempt — gear, a Relic, your footing at the worst moment.
6. Your Stress escalates immediately by 1. The Beast argued, and you listened.

### SECURE AN ADVANTAGE — Miss
1. Your preparation is discovered, and now they're prepared for *you*.
2. You've prepared for the wrong thing entirely. The threat isn't what you assumed.
3. Time you didn't have is gone. The window has closed or is closing.
4. What you set up works, but for someone else's benefit.
5. A resource is consumed for nothing — materials, a favour, a Relic's use.
6. Someone you involved in the setup is now exposed by it.

### GATHER INFORMATION — Miss
1. You learn something true, terrible, and about yourself.
2. The information is deliberately false, and you believe it.
3. Your asking gets back to the wrong person immediately.
4. The source is unwilling, gone, or dead — and recently.
5. You find a body, a scene, or evidence of something worse than what you were investigating.
6. You learn it, but the knowing costs you: Stress +1, or a Connection's trust.

### COMPEL — Miss
1. They refuse, and they tell someone who matters what you asked for.
2. They agree, and they're lying. You won't find out until it matters.
3. You've frightened them badly enough that they do something desperate.
4. You get what you asked for and it's the wrong thing entirely.
5. They counter-offer, and the terms are worse than you'd have accepted an hour ago.
6. Something in how you asked showed them what you are. Exposure +1.

### SLIP AWAY — Miss
1. You're caught. Whatever that means here, it happens.
2. You escape, but not everyone does. Someone with you is taken or left.
3. You get out and leave a clear trail. They'll be along shortly.
4. You escape into somewhere worse than what you were fleeing.
5. You drop, break, or abandon something you needed.
6. You're seen escaping in a way no human could. Exposure +1.

### AID ANOTHER — Miss
1. You get in their way. They roll at −1 and blame you, correctly.
2. Your help exposes you both to something neither of you saw.
3. You take the consequence that was meant for them, in full.
4. What you offered wasn't yours to offer. Someone else pays for it.
5. They see something in how you helped that changes how they see you.
6. Your Stress escalates by 1. Helping cost you something you were holding together.

### UNDERTAKE A JOURNEY — Miss
1. You're intercepted en route by someone who knew you'd come this way.
2. The route is closed, collapsed, claimed, or watched. You need another.
3. You arrive, but far later than you needed to. Something has already happened.
4. Supplies, transport, or a companion are lost along the way.
5. You're followed for the entire journey and only realize at the end.
6. The journey itself marks you. Take a Condition, or Stress +1.

### FACE DEFEAT — Miss
1. You wake somewhere else, held, with time missing.
2. You lose the thing the fight was about, permanently.
3. Someone who was relying on you pays for the loss instead of you.
4. Your defeat is witnessed, and the story spreads. Exposure +1.
5. Take a Grave Condition. Something isn't coming back.
6. Take the **Doomed** or **Indebted** Burden. Whoever beat you now owns something of yours.

## Beast and Recovery Moves

### SATE THE BEAST — Miss
1. You drain them dry. Whatever you meant to do, this is what you did.
2. You attack the wrong target — someone who wasn't prey, and might have been a Connection.
3. You feed and it isn't enough. Stress +1 instead of decreasing.
4. There's a witness, and they got away, and they were recording.
5. You come back to yourself somewhere else, later, with no memory of the interval.
6. You're interrupted mid-feed by something that now knows exactly what you are.

### STEEL THE RESOLVE — Miss
1. The person you reached for isn't there, or won't answer.
2. Your safe place isn't safe anymore, and you find out by arriving.
3. The routine fails. Whatever you use to hold yourself together stops working tonight.
4. You say more than you meant to, to someone who shouldn't have heard it.
5. An old Condition, wound, or grief resurfaces at full strength.
6. You do something to feel steady that you'll have to answer for later.

### RESISTING THE CHANGE — Miss
1. You turn, fully, in the worst available place.
2. You turn, and you're conscious for all of it and in control of none of it.
3. You hold, barely, and take a Troublesome Condition doing it.
4. You turn and hurt someone. The GM decides who — it should be someone who matters.
5. You lose the night. You wake with the change spent and no account of it.
6. Someone sees the change happen. Exposure +2.

### SURRENDER TO THE YEARS — Miss
*(In addition to the Burden and immediate threat the move already specifies.)*
1. Your beast built something in your absence — a following, a reputation, a body count.
2. Someone you loved spent the missing years looking for you, and stopped.
3. A Haven or Relic of yours was taken and is now held by someone who knows what it is.
4. You're wanted — by mortal authorities, your own Kind, or the Vigil.
5. Someone you turned, made, or bound was left to survive alone. They did, and they remember.
6. A Connection you burned during the lost years is now an antagonist with a name.

---

## Skirmish and Conflict

### RESOLVE THE SKIRMISH — Miss on any roll
1. You're hit hard. Take a Troublesome Condition.
2. You lose your footing, your weapon, or your position.
3. An ally is exposed, hurt, or separated from you.
4. Reinforcements, witnesses, or authorities arrive.
5. The environment turns — fire, collapse, water, alarm, sunrise.
6. Your control slips. Stress +1, and everyone present saw something.

### THE MAKING — Miss
*(In addition to the failed turning the move already specifies.)*
1. What comes back is fast, feral, and gone into the city before dawn.
2. They come back wrong and *aware* of it, and they blame you correctly.
3. They don't come back at all, and someone was expecting them home.
4. It works, but something else came through with them.
5. Your own Kind's authority learns of it within the night.
6. You feel a piece of yourself go into the making. Beast +1, beyond the standard cost.

## Masquerade and Recovery

### CLEAN UP — Miss
1. The evidence has already been copied, uploaded, or handed on.
2. Your cleanup is itself witnessed, and it looks worse than the original incident.
3. The person you paid, threatened, or trusted talks anyway.
4. You destroy the wrong thing — something you needed goes with it.
5. Mortal authorities log your interference. City Heat +1.
6. Someone with authority over you learns you've been cleaning up after yourself.

### CLEAR A CONDITION — Miss
1. It worsens. Raise the Condition's rank by one step.
2. Infection, complication, or rejection. Take a second, related Condition.
3. Healing requires something you don't have and will have to go get.
4. Whoever treated you asks a question you can't answer, or wants something.
5. It heals wrong. The Condition becomes Permanent as a cosmetic or minor lasting mark.
6. You push through too early and undo the progress entirely. Clear the track.

### FULFIL YOUR VOW — Miss
1. You were working from a false premise. Someone fed you the wrong thing, deliberately.
2. You've achieved it, and it doesn't fix what you thought it would fix.
3. Someone else completed it first, and took what you wanted from it.
4. The Vow was always bigger than you understood. Raise its rank by one.
5. Fulfilling it requires something you swore you wouldn't do.
6. The person you swore it for no longer wants it, or is no longer there to want it.

### REPAIR A BOND — Miss
1. They say the thing neither of you has said, and it's true.
2. They've already moved on and didn't tell you.
3. They ask you for something you can't give and won't lie about.
4. They've learned what you are, or what you've done. Exposure +1.
5. The Connection drops a rank instead of holding.
6. They agree to everything, warmly, and mean none of it.

---

## When You'd Rather Not Roll

These tables are a tool, not an obligation. If you already know exactly what a miss means — because the fiction has been building to it for three scenes — say it and move on. The oracle is there for the moments when the dice have outpaced your imagination, which happens to everyone, and which is not a failure of GMing.

The one rule: **never let a miss cost nothing.** If you can't find a consequence on the table or in your head, the fallback is always Stress +1. The Beast is always available, and it is always hungry.

# APPENDIX A: EUROPE BY NIGHT
### A Blood & Bone Setting Supplement

## Introduction: The Old Continent

Europe has been dying and being reborn for two thousand years, and the things that walk it after dark have watched every cycle. This is a continent where the borders between nations shift every generation or two, but the borders between Kinds are older, harder, and drawn in blood. A Vampire's territory in Vienna was already old when the Habsburgs were young. A Werewolf pack in the Carpathians remembers when the forests covered ground that's now farmland. Ghosts haunt cities that have been leveled and rebuilt three times over, tethered to buildings that no longer exist.

This supplement gives you six major cities to set your chronicle in, a Europe-wide political overview, and enough factions, threats, and hooks to run months of play in any one of them — or to send your characters travelling between them, carrying trouble across borders the way monsters always have.

## The State of the Night

Three continent-spanning forces shape supernatural politics in modern Europe. Every city write-up in this book references at least one of them.

**The Concord** — An unofficial, frequently-broken non-aggression pact between the older Kinds (particularly Vampires and Werewolves) in cities where open warfare would be too costly to hide from mortals. The Concord isn't a government; it's closer to an understanding, city by city, enforced by whoever's strong enough to enforce it. Breaking it doesn't bring legal consequences — it brings retaliation.

**The Vigil** — A loose, informal network of Hunters across the continent who share information, safehouses, and occasionally resources, without any central leadership. Some cells are religious orders centuries old; others are three friends who found out the truth last year and can't look away. The Vigil doesn't trust easily, and infighting between cells is as common as cooperation.

**The Hollow Roads** — Old smuggling and travel routes — literal and supernatural — that predate most modern borders. Originally trade routes, they're now used by every Kind to move people, secrets, and bodies across Europe without official notice. Controlling a stretch of Hollow Road is enormously valuable and enormously dangerous, since it puts you in everyone's business at once.

## LONDON

**Atmosphere:** A city of layers — modern glass towers built over Victorian sewers built over Roman foundations. Everything old in London is still down there somewhere, and so is everything that used to live in it.

**Dominant Presence:** Vampires hold the financial districts, running the city's oldest money through shell companies that have existed since before shell companies had a name. Ghosts are unusually numerous and unusually organized, thanks to centuries of plague pits, fires, and Blitz casualties that never quite left.

**Key Faction — The Undersett:** A council of the city's eldest Vampires, named for the old buildings they claim as their bolt-holes below street level. The Undersett enforces the Concord ruthlessly in London — not out of principle, but because the city's mortal surveillance infrastructure (cameras on every corner) makes discovery catastrophic for everyone. They've been known to destroy their own kind for masquerade breaches faster than any Hunter could.

**Local Threat:** A Wendigo has been operating in London for six months, feeding carefully on the city's enormous homeless population — people who are missed by no official system. The Undersett hasn't acted because the Wendigo hasn't broken the Concord technically, but the body count is drawing Vigil attention.

**Story Hooks:**
- A Ghost tethered to a demolished plague pit resurfaces when construction crews break ground on a new development, and the tether is now attached to a corporate office building full of the living.
- The Vigil cell operating in London has identified the Undersett's meeting location and is planning a raid — someone needs to warn them, or stop the raid, or make sure it succeeds.

## PARIS

**Atmosphere:** Paris performs elegance the way a predator performs stillness. Every arrondissement has its own supernatural character, from the tourist-thronged center to the catacomb-riddled outskirts where six million dead residents outnumber the living.

**Dominant Presence:** Witches and Warlocks cluster here, drawn by centuries of accumulated occult tradition and a genuine market for magical services among Paris's wealthy and desperate alike. Werewolves control the outer banlieues, where police presence is thinner and territory is easier to hold.

**Key Faction — L'Atelier:** A loose coven-of-covens, more trade guild than cabal, that regulates magical practice in the city. L'Atelier doesn't care what you do with power — only that you pay your dues (literal and Ledger-based) and don't drag mortal attention onto the trade. Crossing L'Atelier means every witch in the city suddenly finds a reason not to help you.

**Local Threat:** The catacombs beneath Paris have become contested Hollow Road territory. Something ancient has taken up residence in an unmapped chamber, and both a Vampire faction and a Werewolf pack want it removed — or claimed — before it disrupts their smuggling routes.

**Story Hooks:**
- A member of L'Atelier has been secretly binding Ghosts as magical batteries, violating every unspoken rule about how the Kinds treat each other — and the Ghosts are starting to fight back.
- A mortal true-crime podcaster has stumbled onto a pattern of disappearances that traces directly back to a Werewolf dominance challenge gone wrong.

## PRAGUE

**Atmosphere:** Prague never really left the Gothic behind. Spires, alchemists' workshops turned into tourist shops, and a castle that's been a seat of power for a thousand years — the city wears its supernatural history openly, which paradoxically makes it easier to hide in.

**Dominant Presence:** Gargoyles are more numerous and more organized here than almost anywhere else in Europe, thanks to the sheer quantity of Gothic and Baroque architecture offering places to disappear into stone. Vampire presence is old but fractured, split among several rival bloodline-courts that have never fully reconciled.

**Key Faction — The Watching Stone:** A council of Prague's Gargoyles, unusual for being one of the only Gargoyle political structures in Europe with any real cohesion. They've traded their traditional isolation for mutual protection, coordinating territory and warning each other about threats. Mortals who study the city's architecture too closely sometimes vanish.

**Local Threat:** A rival Vampire court has hired a Hunter cell — unknowingly working for monsters — to eliminate a competing bloodline, using the Vigil's own people as a weapon in a vampire succession dispute.

**Story Hooks:**
- A tourist livestreams footage that clearly shows a Gargoyle mid-transformation before the Watching Stone can intervene, and it's already been clipped and shared thousands of times.
- One of the rival Vampire courts wants to end the fractured infighting for good and is quietly recruiting outside help — including, possibly, the player characters.

## ROME

**Atmosphere:** Rome doesn't have supernatural history so much as it *is* supernatural history, layered so thick that half the city's monsters don't fully understand what's buried beneath their own territory.

**Dominant Presence:** Vampires with genuinely ancient lineages treat Rome as something close to sacred ground, and the Church's historical entanglement with the supernatural means the Vigil here is unusually institutional — several cells operate with quiet, unofficial blessing from within the Vatican itself.

**Key Faction — The Curia Umbrae ("Shadow Curia"):** A secretive body within the Church, separate from any official Vatican structure, that has tracked and managed supernatural activity in Rome for centuries. They don't hunt indiscriminately — they catalog, contain, and occasionally recruit. Most Kinds in Rome know better than to draw their attention.

**Local Threat:** A newly-turned Vampire, unaware of the Curia Umbrae's existence, has been feeding recklessly near the Vatican itself, and the Curia is running out of patience for the local Vampire elders to handle it internally.

**Story Hooks:**
- A Hunter working within the Curia Umbrae begins to question whether the organization's "containment" is really just another form of predation.
- Catacombs beneath a minor basilica turn out to be a Ghost's tether — one who died believing themselves a saint, and whose fading grip on that belief is becoming dangerous.

## BERLIN

**Atmosphere:** A city rebuilt from rubble twice in one century, Berlin has a rawness other European capitals lack. Its supernatural community is younger, less bound by tradition, and more willing to break old rules.

**Dominant Presence:** Werewolf packs here are unusually egalitarian compared to elsewhere in Europe, having rebuilt their structures from scratch after WWII and the Cold War scattered the old hierarchies. Hunters operate with less centralized structure too — Berlin's Vigil presence is a patchwork of small, independent cells that don't always know about each other.

**Key Faction — The Ninth Ward:** Not a supernatural faction at all, but a mortal underground network — former Stasi informants, hackers, and fixers — who've figured out enough of the truth to quietly profit from it, selling information to whichever Kind pays best. They're dangerous precisely because they don't care about the Concord, the Vigil, or anyone's masquerade.

**Local Threat:** The Ninth Ward has acquired documentation proving a Werewolf pack's involvement in several deaths mortal police have written off as animal attacks, and they're auctioning it to the highest bidder — Vampire courts, rival packs, and at least one Vigil cell are all interested.

**Story Hooks:**
- A young pack member wants to expose the old Cold War-era supernatural cover-ups to the public, believing transparency is safer than secrecy — and the rest of the pack has to decide whether to stop them.
- The Ninth Ward approaches the player characters directly with an offer too useful to easily refuse.

## VIENNA

**Atmosphere:** Vienna carries itself with imperial memory even though the empire is long gone. Old money, older grudges, and a supernatural aristocracy that still behaves as though titles mean something.

**Dominant Presence:** Witches and Warlocks with generations-deep family lineages treat magic as inheritance as much as practice, and Vienna's Vampire population is dominated by a handful of old, wealthy bloodlines who intermarry (in every sense) with mortal high society.

**Key Faction — The Salon:** An exclusive social circle that functions as Vienna's real seat of supernatural power, blending old Vampire nobility, Witch dynasties, and enough sympathetic mortal aristocrats to make outsiders unsure who's what. Invitation to the Salon is currency; exclusion from it is a death sentence dressed as a snub.

**Local Threat:** A Salon member has been secretly Indebted to a foreign faction for decades, and the debt is finally coming due in a way that threatens to expose the entire Salon's carefully maintained facade.

**Story Hooks:**
- A mortal debutante, newly introduced to the Salon's world, has to decide whether to run or stay — and the player characters may be the ones who have to make that choice for her.
- A Witch dynasty's Ledger has been quietly manipulated by a rival family, and the coming reckoning could burn down half of Vienna's supernatural high society.

## Running a Europe-Spanning Chronicle

If your table wants to move between cities rather than settle in one, use the Hollow Roads as your connective tissue — a group with access to (or conflict over) a stretch of Hollow Road has a built-in reason to travel. Consider these campaign throughlines:

- **The Concord Is Breaking:** Something is causing simultaneous Concord violations across multiple cities. Is it coordinated? Who benefits?
- **A Vigil Purge:** A powerful, disciplined Vigil faction is moving city to city, more organized and more successful than anything local Kinds have faced before.
- **The Ledger Comes Due:** A Warlock centuries old has debts spread across half the continent, and they're calling in every favor at once before something catches up to them first.

Each city in this book can stand alone or link into any of these — pull whichever threads suit your table.

# APPENDIX C: QUICK REFERENCE

*One page for the table.*

## The Action Roll
Roll **1d6 (Action Die) + stat + adds** against **2d10 (Challenge Dice)**.
Adds cap at **+2**. Action Score caps at **10**. **Ties go to the Challenge Dice.**

- Beat **both** → **Strong Hit** — you're in control.
- Beat **one** → **Weak Hit** — success with a cost.
- Beat **neither** → **Miss** — Pay the Price.
- **Matched Challenge Dice** → something notable happens, good or bad.

## Don't Roll If
- The outcome isn't genuinely in doubt for this character.
- You already rolled for this beat of action.
- Failure would be arbitrary rather than interesting.

## Generic Moves
| Move | Roll |
|---|---|
| Face Danger | best-fit stat |
| Secure an Advantage | best-fit stat |
| Gather Information | +Wits |
| Compel | +Heart or +Shadow |
| Slip Away | +Nerve |
| Face Defeat | +Heart |

## Skirmish — Rule of 3
State your objective. Make **exactly three** Action Rolls. Track Advantage:
**Strong +1** · **Weak 0 and a cost** · **Miss −1 and Pay the Price**

- **+1 or more:** Decisive Victory
- **0:** Success at a Cost — choose: Feed the Beast / Collateral Damage / The Mask Slips / Flesh and Bone
- **−1 or less:** Defeat → Face Defeat

*Formidable+ enemies survive a Decisive Victory. Mark progress instead.*

## The Meters
| Meter | Range | At Cap |
|---|---|---|
| **Stress** | 0–6 | Time Skip → Surrender to the Years |
| **Beast** | 0–10 | Character lost permanently → Successor |
| **Exposure** | 0–6 | Burned — take Doomed, leave the city |

**The Reckoning** — end of every session: roll **1d6 per point of Stress**. Each **1** advances the Beast Meter.

## Recovery
- **Sate the Beast** — +Shadow (covert) or +Bone (force). Strong −2 Stress · Weak −1 and a complication · Miss Pay the Price.
- **Steel the Resolve** (Hunters) — +Heart or +Wits. Same outcomes.
- **Archetype Stress Relief** — once each per session, −1 Stress, never below 1.
- **Clean Up** — once per session. Strong −2 Exposure · Weak −1 and a cost · Miss +1 and Pay the Price.

## Progress
| Rank | Per mark |
|---|---|
| Troublesome | 3 boxes |
| Dangerous | 2 boxes |
| Formidable | 1 box |
| Extreme | 2 ticks |
| Epic | 1 tick |

Ten boxes, four ticks each. **Fulfil Your Vow / Clear a Condition:** count **filled boxes only** as your score, roll the Challenge Dice.

## Conditions
Each applies **−1** to affected rolls. **Three maximum** — a fourth means **Broken**.

## Misses
Every move has a d6 miss oracle in **Chapter 16**. On a matched miss, roll twice. If you can't find a consequence, the fallback is always **Stress +1**.

## Downtime
Two actions between sessions, *after* the Reckoning: Mend · Lay Low · Repair a Bond · Work the Vow · Fortify · Feed Carefully · Reach Out.

## XP
**Earn:** Vow fulfilled (1–5 by rank) · Kind triggers · Archetype triggers. Each trigger once per session. Expect **3–6 per session**.
**Spend:** new asset **2** · next ability **3** · Connection +1 rank **3** · stat +1 **10** (max 4).

# APPENDIX D: THE HOLLOW HOUR
### A Starter Scenario

*One night. Troublesome-to-Dangerous rank. Playable in a single session with any Cohort and any mix of Kinds.*

## The Situation

Eleven days ago, a body was found in the service tunnels beneath a disused Underground station. Mortal police wrote it up as an overdose. It wasn't — the body had been drained, badly and messily, by something that didn't know what it was doing.

Four more have followed. The city's Heat has climbed to **3**. Local factions are furious, because a sloppy predator is everyone's problem.

**What's actually happening:** a mortal woman named **Runa Sandhu** was turned six weeks ago and abandoned by whoever made her. She has no idea what she is, no one to teach her, and no control. She is not evil. She is nineteen, terrified, and starving, and she has been sleeping in the tunnels because sunlight hurts.

**The complication:** a Vigil hunter, **Callum Reyes**, has been tracking the deaths for a week and is one night from finding her. He is not wrong about what she's done. He is not going to ask questions first.

**The other complication:** the local vampire authority has issued a quiet instruction — *fix this before Heat hits 4* — and does not care how.

## The Hook

Any of these gets the Cohort moving:

- Their Haven, territory, or feeding ground is in the affected district, and the attention is becoming intolerable.
- A Connection of theirs is a person of interest in the mortal investigation.
- They're instructed to handle it by whoever they answer to (Cell, Debt, or Crew Cohorts).
- One of them recognizes the pattern as *someone new and unsupervised*, which is a specific and dangerous problem.

## The Three NPCs

**RUNA SANDHU** — *Troublesome rank. Wants: to stop being hungry, and to be told what she is.*
Feral, fast, and completely untrained. She'll flee before she'll fight, and she'll fight before she'll be captured. She has no idea her maker abandoned her — she thinks she's been left behind by accident and will come back for her.
*What she can do that the players can't:* nothing. That's the tragedy. She's simply strong, fast, and unable to stop.

**CALLUM REYES** — *Dangerous rank. Wants: the killing to stop tonight.*
Vigil, competent, well-supplied, and working alone. He has a location he's narrowed to three tunnels and a schedule. He will listen to a Hunter character. He will not listen to a monster.
*What he can do that the players can't:* he can move openly in daylight and through mortal institutions, and he's already got the police file.

**MARGOT SEVERIN** — *Formidable rank. Wants: Heat down, and a demonstration that she's the one who arranged it.*
The local authority who issued the instruction. She will not appear in person unless the Cohort makes her. She rewards efficiency and punishes attention.
*What she can do that the players can't:* she can make the mortal investigation stop entirely, and she can make the Cohort's problems permanent.

## The Question

The scenario has no correct answer. It has four:

1. **Kill Runa.** Fast, clean, satisfies Margot, drops Heat. Every character who takes part increases their Beast Meter by 1.
2. **Teach her.** Someone takes responsibility for a fledgling who will be dependent, dangerous, and visible. Whoever does gains a Companion and a permanent Connection — and the deaths were still real.
3. **Hand her to Callum.** Neat, deniable, and it means trusting a hunter with a monster. It also tells the Vigil the Cohort is willing to deal.
4. **Find her maker.** The hardest option and the only one that solves the actual problem. This is a Formidable-rank thread that turns a one-shot into a campaign.

## Running It

- **Open in motion.** First scene: the Cohort is already in the tunnels, or already being told to deal with this. Don't play out the decision to get involved.
- **Runa should be sympathetic before she's dangerous.** Let them find where she's been sleeping — a nest of stolen coats, a phone with 200 unanswered messages from her mother — before they meet her.
- **Callum arrives at the worst moment.** Ideally while the Cohort is deciding what to do with her.
- **Heat is the clock.** Every masquerade breach pushes it toward 4, at which point Margot stops being an off-screen instruction and becomes an on-screen problem.

## Progress Tracks

| Objective | Rank |
|---|---|
| Find Runa before Callum does | Dangerous |
| Resolve the situation without raising Heat | Dangerous |
| Identify and locate her maker | Formidable |

## What It Teaches

Run this first because it demonstrates the whole game in one night: Stress pressure while working, a Skirmish that shouldn't be a Skirmish, an Exposure risk in every choice, a Beast Meter cost attached to the easy option, and an ending with no clean version.

If the Cohort walks away having killed a scared teenager to keep the peace, the game worked. If they walk away having adopted her, the game also worked, and now they have a problem with a name.

# APPENDIX E: GLOSSARY

**Action Die** — The single d6 rolled on an action roll, added to a stat and any adds.

**Action Score** — Action Die + stat + adds. Capped at 10. Compared separately against each Challenge Die.

**Adds** — Situational bonuses to an action roll. Capped at **+2 total** regardless of how many sources apply.

**Archetype** — How a character carries their Kind. Two per Kind, each bending the Kind's rules and adding XP triggers, Stress Relief options, and exclusive Powers.

**Assets** — Purchasable capabilities with three tiered abilities: Powers, Paths, Havens, Companions, Relics, and Rites. First ability 2 XP, each further ability 3 XP.

**Beast Meter** — A permanent 0–10 track measuring how much of yourself you've lost. Never resets. At 10, the character is gone.

**Broken** — The state of carrying a fourth Condition. Not dead, but out of the scene and at someone's mercy.

**Burden** — A lasting obligation or curse (Doomed, Indebted) that must be actively resolved.

**Challenge Dice** — The two d10s rolled against your Action Score. Ties go to the Challenge Dice.

**Cohort** — The group premise: why these characters are together. Chosen at Step Zero of character creation.

**Condition** — A lasting injury or impairment applying −1 to affected rolls. Three maximum.

**Connection** — A person, place, or faction you can call on, ranked Weak / Steady / Strong. Can be Burned for an automatic strong hit.

**Downtime** — Two narrated actions between sessions. Occurs *after* the Reckoning.

**Exposure** — A 0–6 track of how much evidence of you personally exists. At 6, you're Burned.

**Heat** — A city-wide 0–6 track of mortal institutional attention, shared by everyone.

**Kind** — What you are: Vampire, Gargoyle, Wendigo, Werewolf, Witch/Warlock, Hunter, or Ghost.

**The Making** — The move for deliberately turning a mortal into your own Kind.

**Match** — Both Challenge Dice showing the same number. Something notable happens beyond the normal outcome.

**Miss** — Your Action Score beats neither Challenge Die. You fail or must concede seriously. Usually Pay the Price.

**Move** — A rule triggered by fiction. Action Moves use the action roll; Progress Moves resolve from a track.

**Paying the Price** — The consequence attached to a miss.

**Progress Track** — Ten boxes of four ticks each, used for Vows, Conditions, journeys, and ranked enemies.

**Rank** — Difficulty on a five-step ladder: Troublesome, Dangerous, Formidable, Extreme, Epic. Sets how much progress each mark yields.

**The Reckoning** — End of every session: roll 1d6 per point of Stress. Each 1 advances the Beast Meter.

**Rule of 3** — Skirmish resolution. Exactly three action rolls, tracking Advantage.

**Sate the Beast** — The recovery move for all Kinds but Hunters. +Shadow or +Bone.

**Steel the Resolve** — The Hunter's recovery move. +Heart or +Wits.

**Stress** — A 0–6 track of immediate pressure. At cap, triggers a Time Skip.

**Stress Relief** — Archetype-specific minor recovery. Once each per session, −1 Stress, never below 1.

**Strong Hit** — Your Action Score beats both Challenge Dice. You're in control.

**Successor** — Rolling a new character connected to one lost to the Beast, who becomes an Epic-rank antagonist.

**Time Skip** — Removal from play for months or years when Stress caps. Resolved with Surrender to the Years. Survivable.

**Vow** — A ranked, tracked goal. Fulfilled with a progress roll; forsaking one costs 1 Stress.

**Weak Hit** — Your Action Score beats one Challenge Die. Success with a cost.

**Weakness** — Each Kind's specific, exploitable vulnerability.
